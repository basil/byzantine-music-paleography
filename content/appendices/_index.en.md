# Appendix 1. Glossary with names of signs and theseis in Byzantine, post-Byzantine, and more recent theorists1

## 1. Prolegomena

The contemporary scholar of musical manuscripts of the Old Method often turns to the old
theoretical writings, in order to find there a point of departure for understanding the highly complex psaltic science
and art of past centuries.2 Since, however, music-theoretical manuals “usually constitute
an aid to the oral tradition or explanations for difficult problems of practice”,3 it is
necessary to study them in conjunction with the musical manuscripts, and with the aid of the present-day practice of
Byzantine music.
In dealing with Byzantine and post-Byzantine theoretical writings, three questions arise in particular,
which are closely connected with one another:4
To what extent do the surviving theoretical writings constitute a single tradition?5
To what extent can the various theoretical writings be correlated with particular evolutionary phases of
Byzantine notation?
To what extent can later theoretical writings be used to explain
questions concerning earlier musical manuscripts?
For the transmission of the notation, the theorists chiefly chose three forms of written material:

1. Lists of neumes, which contain the names and/or graphic forms of the signs (see, for example, the list

of “melodēmata” in the Sticherarion Lavra Γ 67, fol. 159r),

1. exercises in cheironomy and didactic poems, in which the names, graphic forms, and

characteristic formulae of signs appear (e.g. the “Mega Ison” of Saint John Koukouzeles),

1. manuals, which contain explanations of various signs (e.g. the theoretical writing of Gabriel

Hieromonachos).
In order to facilitate the summary and combined study of the most important information
found in the three aforementioned kinds of sources, a Glossary was created, which is combined with
a Synoptic Table of the Great Signs (see ch. 10 of the present work, Table 10.3) and
with a Catalogue of Formulae (see Appendix 2). These three instrumenta studiorum will be able to
assist in palaeographical study, as well as in research concerning the musicological analysis
(musico-syntactic, stylistic, etc.) of Byzantine and post-Byzantine musico-poetic texts.

## 2. Content, Structure, Principles, and Issues6

The Glossary contains all the names of signs and formulae that were identified in the following sources
(see Table App. 1.1), with the exception of some special names of phthorai.7
For the present edition, the original version of the Glossary in the German language has been retained. The
individual lemmata contain, as appropriate, the following categories of data:

1. Name (Ger. Name). If there are more names for the same sign or the same formula,

these too are listed as Secondary Names (Ger. Nebennamen) or Special Names (Ger. Sondernamen).
Peculiarities in the spelling and/or form of the names are also noted (Ger. Neben- und/oder
Sonderformen).
Specifiers (Ger. Präzisierungen): e.g. adjectives or adverbs.

1. Meaning (Ger. Bedeutung): The point of departure was the lexicon of Liddell & Scott & Jones. In addition,

a series of other representative lexica of the Greek language of the ancient, Byzantine, and
post-Byzantine periods was consulted. In the case of names that present a broad semantic range, only those
meanings that seemed to us of interest for the musical contexts are mentioned. In the case of
hard-to-understand names, however, as many meanings as possible are given, in order to facilitate
their comprehension. Cases in which names are correlated with acoustic elements or with
gestures are of particular interest.
Names belonging to the same etymological group are mentioned primarily where no direct evidence was found for the
actual name of a sign or a formula. They are also mentioned where etymologically related names
can shed light on the meaning of the name of a sign or formula. In both cases, names of the same
etymological group are placed in square brackets [ ].

1. Relations (Ger. Relationen): This field serves to record meanings of the names under consideration

[or of etymologically related terms] in the fields of Greek

is mentioned very rarely (three times or fewer), then the sources are also indicated (either here under Presence,

only a few theoretical writings, the information is supplemented with the aid of C. Floros’s Universale
Neumenkunde (1970). (If a sign is documented only in musical manuscripts and is not mentioned
at all in the palaeo-Byzantine theoretical writings, then this is indicated in parentheses).
In the case in which a name refers only to a formula and not also to a sign, this
is indicated with an "F" (abbreviation of the German word Formel - formula, thesis: e.g.
laryngisma).
References to the Synoptic Table of the Great Signs (Ger. MgS-

 In the subsection Explanations (Ger. Erläuterungen) the most important references11

from the theoretical writings are entered, that is, passages with specifications, clarifications concerning interpretation-performance,
cheironomy, musical orthography (principles of combining the signs), particularities in the classification
of the neumes, etc.12
The passages that refer to the cheironomy and performance of the voiced signs are presented
together under the oligon and, respectively, under the apostrophos.
The order of the passages and their classification by means of subheadings aims to facilitate
the reader’s orientation and to display the semantic range of the individual
names. The subheadings usually refer to the central idea of a section and cannot
cover all its details.13

At this point, let us mention the problem of convergence-synonymy among various neumes:14

α. It may be complete and evident, on the basis of the shape of the signs or of palaeographical studies (e.g.

epegērma (ἐπέγερμα) - apothema (ἀπόθεμα) or kylisma (κύλισμα) - laimos (λαιμός)).

β. It may be partial, in the case in which broader and narrower neumatic concepts are combined,

as, for example, katabasma (κατάβασμα) - psēphiston (ψηφιστόν) - psēphistokatabasma (ψηφιστοκατάβασμα).

γ. It may be defined as partial convergence, on the basis of palaeographical studies (see, for example, the explanations for

the lygisma (λύγισμα) and the tinagma (τίναγμα) in Floros, 1970, vol. I, pp. 235-237).
In the first case, the neumes are presented together in the Glossary. In the second case, the
narrower concepts are presented together, the broader ones separately. The concepts in the
third case are also mentioned separately.

## 4. Abbreviations and special symbols

The special symbols used in the Glossary are the following:

( ) for explanations, attestations, expansion of abbreviations

[ ] for words of the same etymological family

| and || symbols for the separation of related and, respectively, different elements

/ or

- beside a manuscript abbreviation: more attestations in the specific source

Greek authors, in order to distinguish them from the explanations that derive from

# Appendix 3. Aids for transcription from the New Method into staff notation

The present Appendix aims to facilitate students’ recall of the signs of the New Method.
It also seeks to make transparent the method of transcription used in the present
handbook for the New Method. The transcriptions in this book aim at a more comprehensible
presentation of the material as a whole and at its accessibility also for users who have
a Western European musical education. A transcription of Byzantine music into staff notation can
never replace the original, and the role of transcription always remains auxiliary. Transcription
into staff notation is an important scholarly tool for musicologists, but
chanting must be based on Byzantine notation. The present Appendix does not offer complete
catalogues of signs and modes, but only the most important elements for the present work. For
further information, the reader may consult various theoretical manuals of the New Method.

## 1. Prolegomena

In contrast to the Old System, in which the study of hymns and troparia proceeded according to the triptych
metrophonia (μετροφωνία) - parallage (παραλλαγή) - melos (μέλος), in the New Method the learning process was simplified,
comprising two stages: parallage and melos. Present-day parallage uses the so-called
polysyllabic pitch-names. It is, in a sense, a kind of solfège, inspired by Western
(French/Italian) music-theoretical works of the eighteenth century (Romanou, 2006, pp. 41-42).
The monosyllabic pitch-names arose from the first seven letters of the Greek alphabet, with
which the following seven syllables were formed: Pa Vou Ga Di Ke Zo΄ Ni΄. In Table

P.3.1. the so-called kanonion (κανόνιο) of the first mode appears, with the corresponding moria (72 for the octave

in total, 30 per tetrachord).

Pa Vou Ga Di Ke Zo΄ Ni΄ Pa΄

## 5.4. The Question of Transcribing Microintervals

The researcher may find in the Greek and international bibliography various ways of transcribing the microintervals of Byzantine music onto the
staff. The symbols adopted in the present handbook are also used in contemporary scores of Eastern classical music
and derive from the monograph by M. Mavroeidis, Musical Modes in the Eastern Mediterranean. The Byzantine Echos, the Arabic Maqam, the Turkish
Makam.ix An open comparative list of accidental signs in various studies or from various musicological discussions appears in Table P.3.13.

![Description](image_13771.jpeg)

Table P.3.13. Comparison of symbols for a more faithful transcription of the microintervals of Byzantine music onto the staff: An open list.

An extremely detailed marking of microintervals, with gradations of symbols according to various historical measurements of intervals from the 19th and
20th centuries, was offered by G. Chatzimichelakis (2013, see e.g. p. 458, Table 59).
It must be emphasized, however, that no transcription can render all the subtle intervallic nuances that occur in the
performance practice of chanters, especially in the case of attractions, which constitute pitches of non-fixed pitch height, moving “in motion”
towards a corresponding dominant pitch.x
