Summary
This chapter focuses on issues concerning the manner of teaching in the Old System. More
specifically, it deals with the concept of metrophony (μετροφωνία), that is, the quantitative measurement of individual
intervals, musical lines, and entire pieces, in conjunction with parallage with
polysyllabic pitch names, and with the transition to melos, i.e. the traditional sonic result. The
sources on which this chapter is based consist of theoretical manuals, lists of signs, and
didactic poems. A central place is occupied by the methods “Choros tetradekapyrseutos” and “Theologe
parthene”. In connection with the latter, reference is also made to the question of chromaticism in the practice and
theory of medieval psalmody.

Prerequisite knowledge
Knowledge of the

New Method is required, as well as the study of chapters 7-8.

## 1. Didactic approaches in the New and the Old System

Chrysanthos of Madytos determines in his Introduction (1821) that the learning of pieces in the new,
analytical notation will take place in two steps: 1. Parallage, with the application of the monosyllabic
pitch names to each note sign, and 2. Melos, with the pronunciation of the syllables of the text on each
pitch sign (see Figures 9.1.-2).

### 2.2. Metrophony in Byzantine and post-Byzantine theoretical writings

Etymologically, the word metrophony derives from the verb “to measure” and the noun “voice” (φωνή), which, in the
specific context denotes the interval of a second. In other words, metrophony is the
process of quantitatively determining the intervals of psalmody, with the second as the unit of measurement. As a
result, an interval of four voices corresponds to the interval of a fifth in the theory of
Western European music, the heptaphonia is an octave, etc.
The concept of metrophony is presented in various ways in lists of signs, in didactic poems,
and in theoretical texts.

#### 2.2.1. Metrophony in lists of signs

In lists of signs, the concept of metrophony appears abstractly (for each sign separately) and
takes the following forms:

- Numerical, with the indication of the intervallic value beside or beneath the sign (see Figure

1. , the red circles, and Figure 9.8),

- verbal, with a relevant clarification beside the sign (see Figure 9.9).

#### 2.2.2. Metrophonia (μετροφωνία) in didactic poems

In didactic poems, the concept of metrophonia is combined with two types of exercises:

A. Troparia (usually stichera) that were fashioned into exercises in metrophonia + parallage (παραλλαγή), through

the resolution of all leaps into stepwise movements (metrophonia) and the addition of modal signatures
beneath each ascending or descending voice (parallage). Some of the best-known didactic poems
of this category are Choros tetradekapyrseutos and Theologe parthene, which will
be presented in greater detail below.

B. Exercises in a single mode or in the oktoechos, with ascending and descending movements within intervallic frameworks of one,

two, three, four, seven, ten, and eleven voices. Exercises of this kind may be brief or
very long, the best known being the famous method of Gregory Bounes Alyates Ne houtos oun
anebaine, in plagal fourth mode.

Comparing the three sources above (Figures 9.16-18) with one another, one observes that the
original old sticheron and the exegesis are in the chromatic genus. For the New Method this is apparent
straightforwardly from the martyriai and phthorai. For the Old System, the element of chromaticism (that is,
the presence of an augmented second within the mode) is inferred from the use of the martyriai. A comparison
of the piece in the Old Sticheraria NLG 884 (fol. 37r), A 139 sup. (fol. 23v, year 1341), and Copenhagen NkS
4960 (fol. 22r-v, fourteenth century) shows that all the medial martyriai belong to the family of mode 2.
The use of the martyria for the mesotēs of mode 2 in the old notation implies a structure of
similar diphonies.xvi It follows that the pitch G will be sharpened: the first diphony of the mode, E F G#, will
show a similarity (though not identity) with the second diphony, G# a h.
Nevertheless, the sticheron for parallage in codex NLG 928, fol. 167r-v, uses beneath the
pitch signs of the metrophony the martyriai according to the system of tetraphony, that is, of the trochos. For the pitch
G it sets the martyria . It follows that here the pitch G will be natural.

![Description](image_10681.jpeg)

As mentioned in chapter 8, section 3.5, this contradiction leads us to a very interesting
phenomenon of the old notation, which various theorists such as Chrysaphes the Elder (verses 438-452)
and the Anonymous theorist of MS NLG 968 (fol. 167r) interpret through the antithetical conceptual
pair “from parallage” - “from melos.”xvii During the Byzantine and early post-Byzantine period,
a form of parallage predominates that is based entirely on the system of tetraphony, as
documented by the diagrams of the Composite Trochos, the Tree of Parallage, and the Kanonion
of the Eight Modes. The intervals are diatonic (augmented seconds are absent). Nevertheless, in pieces
belonging to the family of mode 2, the melos requires different, chromatic sonorities, and the martyriai of mode 2
in practical sources usually follow one another not by tetraphony, but by diphony: see Figures 8.47
and 9.19-20.

The frequent alternation of the phthorai of mode 2 and of nenano in the exegesis, although in Middle Byzantine
notation they are entirely absent, is due to the fact that mode 2 in the old sticheraric melos
goes for the complete cadences to its low base, which coincides with the base of the plagal second mode.
In the Old System, the two modes have a common scale (from the pitch E - Vou). In the New Method, the
scales were differentiated, and mode 2 has Di as its high, melodic base and Ni as the low base of the scale,
whereas the plagal second is theoretically constructed from Pa. When the melos in the exegesis proceeds by
diphony, we are in mode 2, and when melodic developments by triphony predominate, we turn toward
the plagal, with pseudo-parallage (Ga = Pseudo-Di and Ni = Pseudo-Pa, where the piece ends).

##### 2.2.2.4. Gregory Bounes Alyates’ “Method of metrophony”: a brief note

The largest and most complicated exercise in metrophony is due to Gregory Bounes Alyates, the last
protopsaltes of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople before the Fall. It has a rich manuscript
tradition, and a host of epithets in the rubrics of the codices emphasize its importance: “very
useful,” “most useful,” “very beautiful.”xx Its incipit is “Ne, thus then ascend, thus
then descend,” and the method was written in the plagal fourth mode, at the same time documenting the
fundamental place of this mode in the teaching of the Psaltic Art.
Based on lists of signs, the eight-mode Great Ison of Saint John Koukouzeles, and
earlier exercises in metrophony, Alyates’ method presents an almost endless palette
of possibilities for measuring intervallic frameworks of one, two, three, four, five, seven, ten, and
eleven phonai. The verbal text beneath the sign-phonai is music-theoretical in character,
clarifying each time the intervallic framework that is traversed. An excerpt is given in

## 5. Conclusions on the concept of metrophonia (μετροφωνία)

In conclusion, we can say that metrophonia in the Old System constituted a highly complex
concept, with various semantic layers:

 In lists of neumes it appears as an indication of the number of voices (intervals of a second) contained in

each vocal sign (ἔμφωνο σημάδι) separately, and in combinations of vocal signs with one another,

 in didactic poems it is presented exclusively with stepwise movements and in combination with

parallage (παραλλαγή), or with a great variety of theseis (formulaic positions) within intervallic frameworks of one up to as many as eleven voices,
having both stepwise movements and leaps, and bearing underlaid text that is either hymnographic or
music-theoretical,

 in theoretical manuals, metrophonia is referred to:
 as the foundation of the Psaltic Art (Gabriel Hieromonachos),
 as a way of tallying the voices of a phrase or a piece for orthographic checking (Akakios
Chalkeopoulos),
 as a method for understanding the structural pitches (phthongoi) — the basic intervallic structure of a piece,
with the aid of a special kanonion (κανόνιο), representing the pitches of the mode as strings, and placing
the sign-voices (σημαδόφωνα) on them along a vertical axis (Anonymous author of MS EBE 968, post-Byzantine period),
 as the simple rendering of the vocal signs of a piece in Middle Byzantine notation, with
a basic rhythm and with the poetic text of the particular troparion or hymn, as
preparation for a slow exegesis (Chrysanthos).
