Summary
The study of Middle Byzantine notation remains at a preparatory stage if it does not proceed as far as the
so-called exegesis (exēgēsis), that is, the traditional manner of decoding the Old Method in
liturgical practice, on the basis of the living voice. The manner of musical rendering of Middle Byzantine notation
at the cantor’s stands of the Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople can be heard through the
tens of thousands of pages of transcriptions by Chourmouzios the Chartophylax and Gregory the Protopsaltes, which
today constitute a golden key to palaeography, since they make accessible to us the melodic
treasures of Byzantium and the post-Byzantine period in the analytical notation of the New Method. The chapter
includes information on the first appearances and several milestones of exegesis in practical sources,
from Balasios the Priest (c. 1670 [Stathis]) down to the Three Teachers, as well as a brief
reference to the development of the theorization of exegesis, from Akakios Chalkaiopoulos (early 16th c.
[Stathis]) to the present. There follows a systematic approach to exegesis, in table form, with an open
typology (brief and slow exegesis or, in the terminology of I. Arvanitis, syllabic exegesis and melismatic
exegesis, the latter with the subcategories brief, slow, and mixed melismatic) and with reference to the corresponding
repertory in which the individual forms of exegesis were applied by the Three Teachers. Various proposals for expanded applications
of the shorter forms of exegesis to old Byzantine repertory are also mentioned. The chapter concludes with various examples, on the basis of which the reader
is also introduced in practical terms to the multifaceted phenomenon of musical exegesis, which constitutes the crowning achievement
of Palaeography.

Prerequisite knowledge
This chapter presupposes the study of Chapters 7-10. In addition, it is recommended to study the following two works, which are of decisive importance for the subject of exegesis and of Byzantine Musical Studies more generally:

- Stathis, Gregorios. (2006). The Exegesis of Old Byzantine Notation and Edition of an Anonymous Treatise from Codex Xeropotamou 357, as well as an Edition of the Musical Art of Apostolos Konstas of Chios from Codex Docheiariou 389. IBM, Studies 2 (6th repr.). Athens.

- Idem. (2016). The Autograph Manuscripts of the Exegesis into the New Method of Notation. Vol. I. The Prolegomena & Vol. II. The Catalogue. Athens: Institute for Byzantine Musicology.

## 1. Defining Musical Exegesis

In Byzantine music, by the term exegesis (ἐξήγησις) we mean the traditional way of decoding Middle Byzantine musical notation, which is stenographic and needs the assistance of oral tradition in order to be transcribed analytically and/or chanted correctly, leading to the corresponding melos, that is, the inherited sonic result.ii
In other words, “exegesis of the old Byzantine notation is the complete writing down, and therefore also the chanting, of the melos that lies within this or that combination of signs: vocal—ascending and descending—on the one hand, and aphonic or great hypostaseis of cheironomy, as they are called, on the other.”iii Exegesis is a technique and at the same time an art, which unfolds at the level of performance and writing, and embraces the practice of psalmody holistically. It concerns not isolated signs, but the full complement of the components of the Psaltic Art: rhythm, intervals, ethos, the meaning of the poetic text, expression, and style.iv

## 2. Some Milestones in the History of Exegesis

### 2.1. Theoretical and practical sources in Byzantine notation. Known exegetes

The theorist who inaugurates the discourse on the more analytical notation of the sonic result
is Akakios Chalkeopoulos, in his Akribologēmata (Ἀκριβολογήματα), at the beginning of the 16th c., with his references to terms
such as exegesis (ἐξήγησις), fully written-out theseis (θέσεις ὁλόγραφες), and explained aporroai (ἀπορροὲς ἐξηγημένες):v see Figure 11.1.

At the level of Greek musical sources of the post-Byzantine period, the earliest surviving exegeses
of complete pieces are by Balasios Hiereus and concern the following two pieces:

- Funeral Trisagion called Athenian, sung in slow style, plagal second mode nenano (ms. Iviron Monastery 1250, fols. 211v-212v, autograph of Balasios, c. 1670): Figures 11.2.- 3,

- Alleluiarion, in protos-barys mode, by the monk Theodoulos.vii

From Balasios Hiereus up to the Reform of the Three Teachers (Constantinople,
1814/15), many names of exegetes are known (more than fifty).
Written exegesis, at the end of the 17th century limited to a few pieces in Greek
manuscripts, developed a strong momentum during the 18th century, with the result that the more analytical notation
of the music, on the one hand, came to concern ever more pieces and entire repertoires, and, on the other,
the notation itself evolved continuously toward greater analyticity.
The formation of Middle Byzantine exegetical notation, as a transitional stage with
successive “steps,”viii is associated with major and minor figures among the exegetes.ix The
best known are the following:

-  Balasios or Balases Hiereus (fl. c. 1670-1700),

-  Ioannes Trapezountios (1736-†1771),

-  Petros Peloponnesios (c. 1735-†1778),

-  Petros Byzantios († 1808),

-  Georgios Kres († 1816),

-  Apostolos Konstas of Chios (c. 1765/70-†1840),

-  Gregory the Protopsaltes (c. 1777-†1821 ).x

The evolutionary course of exegetical notation was completed with the creation of the New
Method of analytical notation at the beginning of the 19th century, with the contribution of the Three Teachers:

- Archimandrite Chrysanthos of Madytos (c. 1770-1843), metropolitan of Prusa (1833- 1843), a pupil of Petros Byzantios, author of the Introduction to the Theory and Practice of Ecclesiastical Music (Paris, 1821) and of the famous treatise Great Theoretikon of Music (Trieste, 1832).

- Gregory (c. 1777-1821), Lampadarios and later Protopsaltes (1819-1821) of the Great Church of Christ, a pupil of Petros Byzantios and Georgios the Cretan, produced a rich exegetical oeuvre and was active in the teaching of the New Method at the Fourth Patriarchal Music School.

- Chourmouzios Georgiou (or Georgios) from Chalki (c. 1770-1840), Chartophylax of the Great Church of Christ, a pupil of Iakovos the Protopsaltes and Georgios the Cretan, is considered “the most important exegete of the new method (...) he transcribed almost all the types of books and all the chants, as many as tradition preserved from the period before the Fall down to his own time (...) his transcriptions today constitute extremely precious keys to the knowledge of the traditional musical material, chiefly that which survived or was born in the years of Ottoman rule.”xi

The exegetical work of the Three Teachers was continued by various pupils of theirs, the
best known being the Athonite monks Matthaios of Vatopedi, Ioasaph of Dionysiou, Nikolaos and Nikephoros
of Docheiariou, Theophanes of Pantokrator, as well as by Apostolos Konstas of Chios.xii
The means by which the reformers
and their continuators produced tens of thousands of exegeses was the new analytical notation, based on:
a new systematization of the oktoechos (οκτωηχία), preserving the inherited aural traditions,
a new way of notating rhythm, through the use of old graphic forms and
the introduction of many others.
The result was a change in the way Psaltic art was taught in the New Method: the triptych
parallage-metrophonia-melos was replaced by the diptych parallage-melos. Metrophonia became superfluous,
since the melos is notated analytically in all the genera and species of composition, and parallage is now based
on the monosyllabic phthongoi.
Nevertheless, the New Method did not pass without objection and reactions in the world of
church chanters of the time. Especially well known is the case of Konstantinos Byzantios (1777-1862), Protopsaltes of the
Great Church of Christ (1821-1855), who continued to chant from books with the Old System of notation until the end
of his life, while at the same time, at the left analogion of the same church, books with the
reformed notation were in use, a fact which, however, did not alter the sonic result.xiii

### 2.2. Early sources in Slavic staff notations

A most important category of practical sources for exploring the early stages and, more generally,
the entire history of the musical exegesis of Byzantine and post-Byzantine repertoire consists of a
series of Anthologies (usually bearing the title Irmoloj - Heirmologion), written in Kievan square
notation (Kyiv square note)xiv and, later, in partes notation, during the period
from the end of the 16th century to the end of the 18th century.xv Two of these codices are entirely
in Greek, others are bilingual (Greek and Slavic, with the use of the Greek or Cyrillic
alphabet): see Stathis (2001a and 2001b), Tončeva (1982), Yasinovskyi (1996), Shkolnik (1998), Makris
(2011 and 2013), Chudinova (2013), Gerasimova-Persydska & Ignatenko (2006), Ignatenko (2016).xvi
Among these manuscripts are the following:

- The Supraśl Heirmologion, 1598-1601, from the monastery of the same name in present-day Poland, kept in Kyiv, Institute of Manuscripts of the Vernadsky National Library of Ukraine, Col. I, no. 5391 (see Makris, 2011, p. 206),

- Codices Sinai 1477 and Petersburg 586, purely Greek codices, which were written around 1700-1720 (Stathis, 2001b, pp. 690-690),

- Ms. slav. 10846 (1676) and Ms. slav. 10845 (1684) from the Central State Library, Bucharest, as well as Ms. slav. 525 (1731/33) from the Library of the Romanian Academy of Sciences, Bucharest. All three mss. come from the monastery of Schitul Mare (today in western Ukraine: see Tončeva, 1982).xvii

- The Oktoechos of Kallistrat, from the Holy Monastery of Dragomirna in Moldavia, Romania (1769), today in the aforementioned Kyiv library, Collection 306, no. 40Π/XVIII-18 (Ignatenko, 2016. Chudinova, 2013, pp. 255-256).xviii

The transcriptions of Byzantine and post-Byzantine compositions included in these manuscripts
contain precise information concerning the melodic line and rhythm of the corresponding
pieces (see Makris, 2011, p. 205). As Stathis (2001b) emphasizes, the transcriptions in question concern “the
substance of the music, the melodic content that is notated summarily by the signs of
Byzantine notation” (p. 690) and constitute indisputable proof of the stenographic character
of Byzantine notation (p. 694), illuminating the evolutionary stages of the written exegetical
tradition of Psaltic art.
As an example, the “oldest dated notation of a Greek
ecclesiastical melos” in Slavic staff notation is cited below, from the Supraśl Heirmologion (Makris, 2011,
p. 206). It is a Cheroubikon in Slavic, with the rubric: “Politikon, third mode, transferred into
the language of the Russians by a patriarchal psaltes in the year ,αφπγ΄ [1583], September 8,
very beautiful.” The Greek psaltes was possibly the famous Theophanes Karykes, the chanting may have
taken place in the Holy Monastery of Pammakaristos during the feast of the Nativity of the Theotokos, and the scribe may
have been the Russian Theodoros Kasianovits, who had spent a period of education in Constantinople
(ibid.): see Figures 11.4-5.

## 3. The elaboration of the theory of musical exegesis

Although a first reference to the term exegesis, in the sense of more analytical musical notation,
is made in the Akribologēmata of Akakios Chalkeopoulos, nevertheless, Apostolos Konstas of Chios may be regarded as the founder of the theory concerning
exegesis, at the beginning of the 19th century.xx
In his Great Theoretikon, Chrysanthos refers to the old way of teaching Psaltic art, based
on the triptych parallage-metrophonia-melos, and to the Byzantine art of cheironomia. For those
interested, he proposes carrying out comparisons between the Old and the New Method, at the level of various
theseis (e.g. for the ouranisma). Comparative studies (juxtapositions) of sources at various evolutionary stages
of Byzantine notation remain to this day the principal method for studying the phenomenon of
exegesis.xxi
With Georgios Violakis (late 19th century) and especially with Konstantinos Psachos, during the second decade
of the 20th century, exegesis gained a central place in musicological discussions. The theory of
exegesis, however, was initially rejected by foreign scholars. Nevertheless, in place of the intense polemic
that prevailed until about 1970, a fruitful dialogue gradually began to take shape between
scholars of different cultural backgrounds, Greek and foreign. Today the major role of oral tradition and the indispensability (ἐκ τῶν οὐκ ἄνευ) of the living voice in
the teaching and decoding of Byzantine notation are now recognized internationally. The list of researchers who
dealt during the 20th and 21st centuries with issues of exegesis from various perspectives is extensive.
By way of indication, one may mention such names as: Simon Karas, Gregorios Stathis, Manolis Chatzigiakoumis,
Georgios Amargianakis, Dimitrios Dragoumis, Ioannis Arvanitis, Thomas Apostolopoulos, Emmanouil
Giannopoulos, Achilleas Chaldaiakis, Dimitrios Balageorgos, Flora Kritikou, Vasileios Vasileiou,
Ioannis Liakos, Michail Stroumpakis, Ioannis Zannos, Fr. Bartolomeo di Salvo, Kenneth Levy, Jørgen
Raasted, Elena Tončeva, Christian Troelsgård, Nicolae Gheorghiţă, Fr. Filotheu Bălan, Fr. Nikolaos Mezēs,
Adrian Sîrbu, etc.xxii

## 5. Examples of exegeses through source comparisons

### 5.1. A Typology of Collations and Some Methodological Issues

Before we proceed to the individual examples, it should be clarified that, apart from the usual
form of manuscript comparison that we have seen up to now, with all the sources, one beneath the other in
chronological order, various other methods of comparison may also be used in musicological research,
depending on the emphasis that the scholar wishes to give to one or another aspect of
the research. More specifically, collations are divided into the following types: see Table 11.2.

|Criteria|Types of collation|Clarifications|
|---|---|---|
|Order of sources|1. Retrospective|The collation begins with the most recent sources and proceeds chronologically backwards, towards the older ones. It was proposed by K. Psachos (1917/1978) as the basic research methodology in Palaeography|
||2. Prospective|The sources are set out in chronological order, from the older to the more recent.|
||3. Stylistic|The sources are grouped according to style of composition, on the basis of their similarities and differences, in order to facilitate their comparison.|
||4. By language|In cases where sources in various languages are compared (e.g. Greek, Old Slavonic, Latin, Syriac, Romanian, etc.), the grouping is usually done by language, because of differences in the number of syllables of the poetic text and, possibly, in the order and/or selection of the musical formulae.|
||Often the retrospective|or the prospective collation may be combined with the stylistic one.|
|Completeness|5. Positive|All the sources are entered in full form.|
|of sources|6. Negative|The best MS is chosen as the dux (in the sense of a guiding MS) for each group of manuscripts (e.g. one dux for the old sticheraric style, one for the new embellishment, and so on). The signs and the text of the guiding MS will be entered in full and will constitute the point of reference for all the other similar sources, for which only the differences will be recorded.|
||7. Mixed|A combination of positive and negative collation. This may be done both in a horizontal direction and in a vertical one. a. In a horizontal direction: see Raasted (1966, tables in A 3 double-page spread, pp. 181-193), where the collation is negative, with the exception of the cadences (cadential formula of the kylisma, martyrias, musico-poetic punctuation, the beginning of the next colon with a confirmatory ison or another sign), where it is positive, since this is also the subject of the book: intonation formulae and martyrias. b. In a vertical direction: the use of negative collation is chosen for some sources, since they resemble the guiding MS very closely. In this way all the differences will immediately be visible, whereas a positive collation would mean, for the reader, a time-consuming process of comparison in order to identify any differences.|

Table 11.2. An open typology of collations in the Palaeography of Byzantine Music.

In the heirmologic and sticheraric repertory, collations can—for the most part—
be made with the sung syllable as the unit, that is, by aligning the neumatic sign-phonemes (σημαδόφωνα) by syllable. Especially
at the beginning, it is very helpful if the syllables are separated from one another by vertical lines, especially in the more
melismatic textures (e.g. in the slow sticheraric melos), where the syllables are anagrammatized or
pseudo-syllables are added, with the gorgic or ephelcystic n. In such cases no additional vertical
lines are needed; rather, we count the syllable from its first appearance up to the appearance of the next
syllable, e.g. Sē sē | me | ro[on |.

In the papadic genre and in other melismatic styles, collations can be aligned by

The typology of collations above is open. Various types may be combined

## 6. More recent investigations

During the twentieth century and up to the present, various musicologists have attempted to trace possible
ways of interpreting the old notation also in a shorter manner, for cases in
which the Three Teachers had recorded only slow exegeseis. Simon Karas proposed, for example, an
exegesis for the Christmas doxastikon by Saint Kassiane, When Augustus ruled alone, in an
argo-syntomon musical texture. As his point of reference he had the form of exegesis that we encounter in the
Doxastarion of Petros Peloponnesios.xxix
Continuing the above efforts, Ioannes Arvanitis (2003, 2006, 2010) proposed a
syllabic transcription-interpretation of heirmologic and sticheraric melē of the old Byzantine
repertoire in duple metre. This is an important tool of musicological research, which helps
in tracing the basic rhythmic structure of the old heirmologic and sticheraric compositions.
It is based on the following principles (open list):

1. The basic metre is duple. Triple metres are created only exceptionally.

2. The palaeographical unit consists of the sung syllable, not of the isolated sign (cf. Jammers, 1962, p. 51).

3. Syllables may be short (with a duration of 1 primary time-unit) or long (2 or more time-units).

4. All syllables of the text that have one emphonon (ἔμφωνο) sign above them are short.

5. Syllables with a diple, two apostrophoi, syndesmoi, or a kratema are long (with a duration of 2 time-units).

6. Syllables with composite accent signs (e.g. with dyo, apantrichisma, apeso exo, xēron klasma, anastama, etc.) are likewise long, with a total duration of 2 time-units.

7. Syllables with two kentemata last 1 time-unit (two eighth notes in the transcription).

8. Syllables with the bareia and two emphona signs last in total 1 time-unit (the bareia is one of the great signs of the group of synagmata, which gather several pitches into one time-unit: see Akribeia [ed. Schartau], lines 814-826. Karas, 1982, vol. I, p. 55).

9. An emphonon sign with a klasma also lasts one time-unit, as do two apostrophoi written obliquely and with a klasma (the so-called rapisma).

10. The duration of the apoderma may vary, depending on the context (2 or 3 time-units, according to the needs of the metre).

## 7. Concluding remarks on the exegesis of Middle Byzantine notation

The study of Middle Byzantine notation remains at a preparatory stage if it does not proceed as far as
the so-called exegesis, that is, the traditional way of decoding Middle Byzantine
notation in liturgical practice, on the basis of the living voice.
The various stages of written exegesis can be studied through a collation of sources of the
Old Method with the New, and with manuscripts in Slavic staff notations (Kievan and
partes). Musicological analysis assists in the study of exegesis: microsyntactic (identification
of theseis), reductive (examination of the exegesis for each sung syllable, with intervallic space and
time as axes), and other forms of analysis.xxxi
From the late tradition of musical manuscripts and theoretical texts it emerges that the great
signs (ΜγΣ) belong to a “musical mental matrix” (musikalische Denkmatrixxxxii), according to which the
relation between the sign (ΜγΣ or emphonon) and what is notated is α : β, where β may depend
on the following elements:

1. the thesis (musical formula) and, possibly, its placement in the musical phrase. Even the
colour of the ink (a black or red aphonon sign) may be significant,

1. the degree, the mode, the genus of the mode,

1. the hymnographic and musical genus, the type and style of melopoeia, and the tempo. These
elements determine the way in which the piece is decoded, that is, the type of exegesis (slow,
brief) of the whole piece.
In contemporary research, even the poetic text and the conditions under which a performance takes place
are considered co-determinative for the interpretation of a piece (that is, whether it is a major feast or
an ordinary weekday, whether the service takes place in a large church or in a small village church, whether it is chanted
soloistically or by a choir),

1. the style (of a school, an era, a chanter).

Consequently, a uniform transcription of pieces in Middle Byzantine notation—whether slow
or brief—that does not take into account the above parameters remains unsuitable from a stylistic
and artistic point of view.xxxiii
It is desirable to investigate how the various aforementioned parameters evolved historically
and how old the Denkmatrix of which we have spoken is.

Byzantine melos is a sister art of Byzantine iconography. The famous Byzantine
musical theseis have been likened to the tesserae of Byzantine mosaics, which, combined with
mastery, render the holy images that traverse the centuries unchanged.
Behind every Byzantine icon there is a drawing which, with faint but sure lines,
sketches out the future image, serving as a guide for the iconographer in the pictorial rendering of the sacred
persons: see Figures 11.32-33.

as a sacred sonic image, from generation to generation.xxxiv
Future research on the phenomenon of exegesis, continuing the studies of the already
rich and multifaceted bibliography, will be able to reveal still other, as yet unknown aspects
of this central subject of the Palaeography of Byzantine Music.

(theoretical methods, kalophonic compositions, notation) supports dating him to the early
