## Summary

This chapter introduces the reader to the study of Old Byzantine notation (10th–12th c.), a chapter of major importance for Byzantine Musicology in particular and for Musicology more generally, since broad layers of the old, classical Byzantine musical repertoire—the Ars antiqua byzantina—are here set down in musical writing for the first time, and this would become a point of reference not only for Greek ecclesiastical music, but also for Latin and Old Slavonic chant. After a brief reference to the major scholars who shaped the field of the systematic study of Old Byzantine notation (J.-B. Thibaut, K. Floros, O. Strunk, J. Raasted, etc.), the two families of Old Byzantine notation are presented, first in summary and then in greater detail: Athonite (Chartres) and Hagiopolitan (Coislin). The study begins with samples of representative manuscripts of the two notational families and continues with the earliest theoretical sources for melodic notations. More specifically, the list of “melodēmata” («μελωδήματα») in ms. Lavra Γ 67, fol. 159r, is presented for Athonite notation, and the corresponding list of signs from the “Hagiopolites” for the notational system of the same name. We then proceed to an overall consideration of the Old Byzantine notations and their individual stages of development, on the basis of studies by K. Floros. The chapter concludes with samples from various manuscripts with Old Byzantine notation and corresponding exercises in reading texts and identifying Old Byzantine notations.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

The study of the preceding chapters (1–5) greatly facilitates understanding of the present chapter.
It is also recommended to study the following book: Floros, Konstantinos. (1998). The Greek Tradition in the
Musical Scripts of the Middle Ages. Introduction to Neumatic Science. Trans.-ed. Kostas Kakavelakis, philol.
ed. N. Avramopoulos. Thessaloniki: Ziti.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1. Preliminary Remarks, Scholars, the Earliest Codices

In the preceding chapter, we saw various musical notations, chiefly of the first Christian
millennium, which had a limited circulation and have survived for the most part only fragmentarily.
By contrast with the primordial forms of Byzantine musical scripts, Palaeo-Byzantine
notation constitutes the first major and coherent graphic musical system of the Byzantines, which
was used for recording troparia and hymns from the end of the first millennium until the
end of the 12th century.
Among the earliest codices with Palaeo-Byzantine notation that have survived down to
the present day are numbered, among others, the following manuscripts:

- The Heirmologia (Ειρμολόγια):

-  Lavra B 32, approximately from the middle of the 10th c. ("not earlier than 950": [Strunk, ed., 1966,

Specimina, Pars Suppletoria, pp. 11-13: 13]/2nd half of the 10th c. [Floros, 1970, vol. I, p. 63]), with 312 folios,

-  Petropolitanus graecus 557, late 10th c. (Floros, 1970, vol. I, p. 64), with 16 folios,

-  Esphigmenou 54, early 11th c. (Floros, 1970, vol. I, p. 64), with 139 folios,

-  Patmos 55, early 11th c. (Floros, 1970, vol. I, p. 64), with 198 folios,

- the Sticherarion Rizov F 1650k/op4/3, in the Central State Archives in Sofia, late 10th c. (see

Atanasov, 2012, p. 84).
The dating of these manuscripts is a difficult undertaking, since they lack a bibliographical
note and the Palaeo-Byzantine notations display many peculiarities, especially
in their early stages. According to O. Strunk, the earliest source is the Heirmologion fragment
Petrop. gr. 557, which became known through a series of facsimiles in J.-B. Thibaut’s Monuments of Ekphonetic and
Hagiopolite Notation of the Greek Church (1913, pp. 65-72 and pls. vi-xxiii). O.
Strunk notes the following with regard to the aforementioned fragment: "Only a little later than the Codex

From the earliest codices to the present day, we have more than a thousand years of uninterrupted use
of Byzantine musical notation, in an enormous number of manuscripts (between seven and ten thousand) and
a multitude of printed editions (from the year 1820 onward). This is a unique trajectory in the world
history of music, distinguished by slow but continuous developments, with the result that the
notation of the New Method in use today constitutes an organic continuation of Palaeo-Byzantine and
Middle Byzantine notation.
Palaeo-Byzantine notation, through which we can go back to the roots of the
hymnographic-musical repertory of the Greek Psaltic Art, while at the same time illuminating other
related musical traditions, has attracted the interest of various scholars from the beginning of the 20th century
to the present day. By way of indication, the following persons and works may be mentioned:

• J.-B. Thibaut, with his work Monuments of the Ekphonetic and Hagiopolite Notation of the

Greek Church (1913),

- H.J.W. Tillyard, with various articles on the Palaeo-Byzantine neumes (e.g. 1937),

- Oliver Strunk, with the monumental Specimina notationum antiquiorum (1966),

- Jørgen Raasted, with the edition of the Palaeo-Byzantine Heirmologion Savva 83 (1968),

- Gerda Wolfram, with the edition of the Sticherarium antiquum Vindobonense (1987),

- Constantine Floros, with the major research undertaking of Universal Neumatic

Science (1970), in which Palaeo-Byzantine, Old Slavonic, and medieval Latin
notations are compared with one another,

• the Proceedings of the three symposia devoted to the Palaeo-Byzantine notations, which

had taken place at Castle Hernen in the Netherlands (Raasted & Troelsgård, eds., 1995.
Troelsgård & Wolfram, eds., 1999. Wolfram, ed., 2004),

• the Proceedings of the Byzantine musicological papers of the 22nd International Congress of

Byzantine Studies in Sofia (Hannick, Troelsgård, Kujumdzieva & Harkov, eds., 2012).

### 1.2. Notational Systems, Chronological Frameworks, and Stages of Development of the Palaeo-Byzantine Notations

Already in its archaic stage, in the Heirmologion Lavra B 32, Palaeo-Byzantine notation appears in
two closely related forms, which were recognized as two families or daughter systems of the same—
still being sought today—archetypal Palaeo-Byzantine parasemantic notation. One family came
to be called Chartres, the other Coislin. The corresponding Greek names are:
Athonite and Hagiopolite notation. The aforementioned French names are each associated
with a representative manuscript from collections in French libraries, while the Greek names
identify the geographical zones where the two neumatic
families most probably developed at first: Mount Athos and Jerusalem–the Holy Land, respectively.vii In addition, comparative studies between
ekphonetic and Palaeo-Byzantine notation have brought to light the close relationship between the two kinds
of musical scripts (13 common signs, according to C. Floros).viii

## 2. Theoretical Treatises of the Old Byzantine Notations

The study of the two Old Byzantine neumatic systems would be extremely difficult if we
could not consult, alongside the practical sources (musical manuscripts), the corresponding
music-theoretical evidence from the same period (tenth-twelfth century). Fortunately, for both Athonite and
Hagiopolite notation, theoretical evidence has survived that allows us today to
identify many of the Old Byzantine signs, to trace the beginnings of the Byzantine oktoechos,
and, more generally, to listen in on the first developments of music-theoretical reflection concerning the

Psaltic Art. The two theoretical treatises are the following:

• List of neumes in the Sticherarion Lavra Γ 67, fol. 159r, with the inscription: With God, the beginnings

of the melodies (early eleventh century [Floros]): Athonite notation (Chartres III),xxiv

• Hagiopolites (date of composition: twelfth century, with later revisions): Hagiopolite notation

(Coislin).xxv
Below we shall examine the sets of Old Byzantine signs and/or theseis (θέσεις; melodic formulae)
that are mentioned in the two theoretical treatises.

### 2.1. Athonite notation (Chartres)

We begin by reading the famous list, which is found at the end of the Lavra Γ 67 Sticherarion, a
codex that contains stichera of the Triodion, the Pentecostarion, and the Oktoechos. The sticheraric
repertoire of this manuscript, which probably comes from Constantinople, includes,
among other things, at least 41 stichera by Emperor Leo VI the Wise (886–912, Figure 6.18), as
well as several compositions (stichera and exaposteilaria) by his son, Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus

(913–959), a fact that led K. Floros to call this particular manuscript a

“royal codex.”xxvi

### 2.2. Hagiopolitan notation (Coislin)

A corresponding list of signs, this time for Hagiopolitan notation, is found in the oldest
surviving manual of the Psaltic Art, which bears the title Book Hagiopolites compiled
from certain musical methods. This anonymous manual, associated with the tradition of the
Holy City (Jerusalem), survives in various versions. Its most complete source is in the manuscript
Parisinus graecus 360, fols. 216r–237r, from the 14th century. However, the theoretical material of the Hagiopolites is
older and varied, referring to Palaeo-Byzantine Hagiopolitan notation, to Middle Byzantine
notation, and also to ancient Greek music theory. This music-theoretical compilation
probably dates to the 12th c.xxix
In §§ 21–22 of the Hagiopolites (ed. Raasted, 1983, pp. 28–30) the following is recorded (original
text in black letters; in blue, some comments from the editor of the text):

§ 21. “The tonoi of the chant are these: isotes, oligon, met’ oligon, meson,
hypermeson, akron, and teleion;”
According to a comment by the editor, what we have here is a mixture of Byzantine and
ancient Greek terms for the seven strings of the seven-stringed instrument (also mentioned
in § 103). The same musicologist notes a similarity to the beginning
of the Lavra Γ 67 list, where seven phonai are discussed, the last likewise being
the “teleia.”
“But according to the <history?> they were later increased <to 15?> by those
who had sufficiently ascertained their power.”
The textual tradition is problematic at this point, but it is likely
that here there is a reference to the creation of the “perfect system.”
§ 22. “Such also are the properties of the phonai among those handed down in the Church
to be sung to God, numbering nineteen
together with the four pneumata and what are called elements; and
their names are these: ison, oligon, oxeia, petaste, kouphisma, bareia,
apostrophos, katabasma, kylisma, anatrichisma, apoderma, antikenoma,
xeron klasma, kratema, syrma. Together with these, the four pneumata:
kentema, hypsele, chamelon, and elaphron. In all nineteen, and three hemitona:
seisma, small klasma, and parakletike.”
This paragraph refers to the signs and/or the theseis (musical
formulas) that accompany the liturgical texts of ecclesiastical music
(the verb prosadesthai is noteworthy) and enumerates a total of 22 names
of signs, recorded in three categories: tonoi (15 in number; this
first category is implied by the context, see § 21 and other versions
of the Hagiopolites), pneumata (4), and hemitona (3).

In the Hagiopolites, then, the first classification of the signs of the
Psaltic Art is also documented. K. Floros called it “Hagiopolitan,” in contrast to the
Middle Byzantine classification, which we shall see in the next chapter.
The Hagiopolitan classification of signs is mentioned in the form of questions and answers and with
a recording of the shapes of the signs in the Protheoria that exists at the beginning of the
fragment Petropolitanum graecum 495, fol. 1v. This MS, known as the
“Sticherarion of Neophytos, hieromonk from Damascus,” dates to the first half of the
13th century (cf. Gertsman, 1994, p. 230)xxx: see Figure 6.20.

## 3. Towards a systematization of the entire set of signs of Paleo-Byzantine notation. Methodological issues

In the previous section we saw the signs of Paleo-Byzantine notation that are mentioned
in the list of neumes of ms. Lavra Γ 67 and in the Hagiopolites. When, however, one studies the practical
sources, one is confronted with many other signs that are not mentioned in the theoretical sources.
Another difficulty for the modern scholar lies in the very nature of Paleo-Byzantine
notation, which is adiastematic. Nevertheless, because the tradition of the Psaltic Art, especially in the
old, classical style (10th-13th c. and later), is very stable, many of the pieces that are
written in Paleo-Byzantine notation can be compared with corresponding sources in
Middle Byzantine notation. The systematic collation of sources led H.J.W. Tillyard and
subsequently other researchers to the conclusion that both the Paleo-Byzantine, adiastematic,
and the corresponding Middle Byzantine, diastematic neumatic renderings encoded (in the overwhelming
majority of cases) the same melodies.xxxii
The many years of combined study of practical and music-theoretical sources of
Paleo-Byzantine notation, in comparison with corresponding theoretical and musical manuscripts of
Middle Byzantine notation, on the basis of the triptych of shape-name-musical meaning of the signs,
that is, from a palaeographical-nomenclatural-semantic point of view,xxxiii led Constantin
Floros to important conclusions regarding:

 the totality of the neumes used in Paleo-Byzantine musical manuscripts, their

graphic forms, their various names, and their multifaceted musical roles; and

 the characteristic elements that mark the evolutionary course of Athonite and

Hagiopolitan notation more generally.
The same palaeographer states:

“When someone has finally fully classified and catalogued all the
Paleo-Byzantine and Old Slavonic neumes, it is impossible to suppress within
oneself the feeling of admiration that spontaneously comes over one, observing
the high-level musical notational technique of Byzantium in its so abundant
and detailed form. One sees it presented panoramically and in its entirety.
In Chartres notation and in Old Slavonic Kondakarian
notation, the number of signs used far exceeds
one hundred.”xxxiv

Before we proceed to consider the entire set of Paleo-Byzantine signs, it is necessary
to say a few things about the individual evolutionary phases of the two Paleo-Byzantine systems
of musical writing.

### 3.1. Distinctive features of the individual evolutionary phases of palaeo-Byzantine notation

Byzantine musical notation, as a living cultural instrument, is in continuous evolution. Moreover,
palaeographical research continually brings to light new sources, enriching our knowledge concerning the
earliest witnesses and the developmental stages of palaeo-Byzantine notation.xxxv We saw in the
previous sections that the course of development of palaeo-Byzantine notation can be divided
into various phases. This is done on the basis of the following criteria:

• The number of syllables of the text that bear no neume above them

(unneumed syllables),

- the graphic shape of the signs,

- specific changes in the number of signs and in the way some signs are written,

that is, “notational-technical transformations,” such as a. the introduction of new signs, b. the clarification of the
meaning of some signs by the addition of other phonetic signs (σημαδόφωνα), c. the more analytical recording
of some stenographic signs (in Athonite notation).xxxvi

## 4. Research methodology

In the preceding sections, historical data and sources of Palaeo-Byzantine notation were presented,
together with corresponding theoretical manuscripts. In addition, summary tables were provided with the
individual characteristics of the evolutionary phases of Hagiopolite (Coislin) and Athonite (Chartres)
notation, as well as a synoptic table with the signs of Palaeo-Byzantine notation, according to the research
of C. Floros.
A classic method for studying a troparion or hymn in Palaeo-Byzantine notation mainly includes the
following working steps:

1. At a very initial stage and/or when the sources are not readily accessible, we can begin

by making a copy of the piece from the manuscript or from a photograph of it: see Figure 6.21.

1. Next, the musico-poetic text is transcribed, with the expansion of abbreviations and

corrections of orthographic errors, and with division into musico-poetic units. This is followed by the identification of the
signs, on the basis of the theoretical texts of Palaeo-Byzantine notation and the corresponding tables
of signs: see Figure 6.22.

1. At a more advanced stage, source collations may follow, that is, the comparison

of several sources that contain the same piece (or its prosomoion),xxxix in various evolutionary phases of
Palaeo-Byzantine notation and in Middle Byzantine notation, and possibly also in Palaeo-Slavic
notations, etc.: see Figure 6.23.
Such comparative studies may be extended chronologically both backwards (sources in primitive
notations, if they exist) and forwards, toward the exegeseis of the corresponding pieces in the
New Method (see chap. 11).

1. In cases where a piece has survived only in Palaeo-Byzantine notation, the experienced

palaeographer may propose a transcription in campo aperto (in an open field), orienting himself
on the basis of the mode of the piece and its dominant pitches, its cadences and other positions
(formulae), which are consistently combined with specific melodic degrees: see Figure 6.24.
