## Summary

This chapter outlines the development of the notation of Greek music from the third century AD to the twelfth century, chiefly on the basis of fragments and local musical notations. Important findings are presented that document various attempts to record pre-Byzantine and Byzantine music, using a variety of graphic systems which, however, for the most part saw limited use and dissemination in the early Christian and Byzantine world. The Oxyrhynchus Papyrus, no. 1786 (second half of the third century AD), with ancient Greek alphabetic vocal notation, the Notation of Hermoupolis, the “Theta” and “Double” notations, as well as other local notations, constitute important evidence for the presence of Greek musical scripts during the first Christian millennium.

## 1. Prolegomena

Few testimonies of practical sources for Greek music have survived from the first Christian millennium.
They are chiefly fragments or monuments of more limited geographical scope, which nevertheless
show the roots from which the great tradition of melodic notations of the second
millennium was born (Palaeobyzantine, Middle Byzantine, and that of the New Method).
On the other hand, during the sixth and eighth centuries there were great flowerings in the field of
Byzantine hymnography, with the cultivation of the kontakion and the kanon.i Since in Byzantine Music
the poetic word and the melos that clothes it constitute an indissoluble unity,ii we may
suppose that the music of those periods too must have undergone corresponding developments, on the basis
of a strong oral tradition.
Below will be presented in summary the famous Oxyrhynchus Papyrus no. 1786 with
ancient Greek alphabetic vocal notation, as well as various primordial notations that are
documented: in early Christian papyri, in fragments of Coptic provenance from the
John Rylands Library of the University of Manchester (Hermoupolis notation), in the palimpsest
codex Garrett 24 from the Firestone Library (Princeton), in various Greek, Slavic, and Syro-Melkite
manuscripts of the eighth to sixteenth centuries (“Theta” Notation, and the related “Double” Notation), in various
Sinaitic manuscripts (Sinaitic notation), in parchment fragments of the Kappadochos archive from the
Department of Music Studies of the University of Athens (Cypro-Palestinian Notation), and in other
sources.iii

## 2. The Oxyrhynchus Papyrus no. 1786 with ancient Greek vocal alphabetic notation

In the ancient Greek city of Oxyrhynchus, which lies on a western branch of the Nile in Upper
Egypt, the oldest written monument of Christian music was found in 1918. The
papyrus in question was assigned the inventory number 1786 and was published for the first time by A. S. Hunt.iv
The Oxyrhynchus Papyrus 1786 is dated to the second half of the 3rd century AD and contains a fragment
of a hymn to the Holy Trinity. It is written in the learned Greek language, in the Doric dialect and in
prosodic metre, more specifically in anapaestic monometers. Above the words of the poetic text,
in majuscule script, symbols can be discerned—the so-called musical signs (σημεῖα μουσικά) or musical punctuation (μουσική στίξις)—of an
ancient Greek system of notation. This is the type of ancient Greek alphabetic vocal
notation, which is based on the Ionic alphabet.v The music recorded in this
fragment is in the diatonic Hypolydian harmonia. Thus, this particular papyrus constitutes not only the
first document of Christian music, but, at the same time, also the last document of ancient Greek
music, owing to its notation and its musical texture.vi In Figure 5.1 and its associated
files this papyrus is presented in its contexts, together with editions, analyses, and performances
of it, in the form of a music-educational presentation.vii

## 3. Early Christian hymns on papyri and ostraca, with elements of primordial notation systems

### 3.1. Some Introductory Remarks

Oxyrhynchus Papyrus 1786, discussed in the previous section, has remained to this day
the only known witness to a Christian hymn with ancient Greek musical notation.
Reference has already been made in chapter 2 of the present handbook to the famous Rylands Papyrus 470, in which, between the words of the hymn, there appear a double
acute accent, two small curved signs, as well as a colon, perhaps connected with the musical performance
or the reading aloud of the Theotokion (θεοτοκίον) (see
Figure 2.46.a).
The journey toward tracing the evolutionary course of Greek music during the first
Christian millennium continues through the careful examination of hymnographic sources on papyri,
ostraca, parchment, and early paper, in which attestations of echoi (modes), special uses
of prosodic signs, or various other symbols such as double or multiple acute accents, double grave accents,
hook-shaped forms, and others appear from time to time (cf. Papathanasiou & Boukas, 2003. Troelsgård, 2007. Sarischouli, 1995).
Below we shall refer briefly to some of the most important finds.

### 3.2. Papyrus Berolinensis 21319viii

A special place among the papyri containing fragments of Byzantine hymnography is also occupied by
Papyrus no. 21319 from the Egyptian Museum and Papyrus Collection of the State Museums of
Berlin. It is of unknown provenance, but, according to its editor, P. Sarischouli, it probably
comes from Egypt, on the basis of certain influences from the Coptic language that can be discerned in the
text. According to the same scholar, the Berlin Papyrus may be dated to the 6th or 7th, or
even to the 8th century. It is written in a careful right-sloping majuscule book hand.ix
It contains fragments of three Theotokion troparia (perhaps stichera or kathismata), which focus
on the Annunciation of the Most Holy Theotokos.
What is noteworthy for the palaeographer of Byzantine music is the signs of musico-poetic
punctuation (middle stops) and especially one of the earliest indications of an initial modal martyría, in combination
with a primitive notation, consisting of dots and hook-shaped forms placed above
some letters (or syllables): see photographs of the original from the Egyptian Museum & the
Papyrus Collection of the State Museums of Berlin (Figures 5.2.a-b) and the diplomatic
transcription and proposed restoration of the text by P. Sarischouli (Figures 5.3-4).

## 4. The notation of Hermoupolisxi

South of Oxyrhynchus, near the banks of the Nile, lay the ancient Egyptian city of Khmoun,
called Hermoupolis in Greek.xii Probably from there, and reflecting the tradition of Egyptian
Christians (the so-called Copts), come five manuscript fragments in Greek
right-sloping majuscule script, which are preserved today in the papyrus collection of the
John Rylands Library of the University of Manchester, as Papyri Rylands Coptici 25recto-29recto.
The five fragments are written on early Eastern paper and contain hymnographic texts in
Greek, concerning the Most Holy Theotokos, the feast of Christmas, and others. The many grammatical
and orthographic errors support the hypothesis that the scribe/scribes
of the manuscripts were not of Greek origin.
The five manuscripts are of particular interest for Byzantine Musicology, since
above the text there appears a musical notation consisting mainly of the sign of the acute,
which may stand alone, double, triple, etc., up to sevenfold.
The musicologists Ioannis Papathanasiou and Nikolaos Boukas, who recognized this
notation as a distinct musical script with local use and named it the notation of
Hermoupolis, date the five manuscripts between the 7th and 9th centuries and propose an intervallic
reading of their notation. The basic principles of the proposed reading are as follows:

- Where there is no musical sign, the base of the mode is rendered,

- the number of acutes indicates the number of voices (intervals of a second) in ascent, always in relation to the

base of the mode, and not measured from the preceding note.

## 5. The Princeton Palimpsestxvii

The palimpsest codex preserved in the Firestone Library, Princeton, in the Garrett Collection, with
no. 24, is of great importance for Byzantine hymnography, since it is perhaps the oldest surviving
example of the Jerusalem Heirmologion in the Greek language, with heirmoi by Saint John
of Damascus, Kosmas the Melodist, Andrew of Crete, Cyprian, as well as several so-called
“Sinaitic” heirmoi. The heirmoi are organized by mode and ode (e.g. first mode: all the heirmoi of the first
ode, then all those of the second or the third ode, etc., up to the ninth ode. The heirmoi of the second
mode, the third mode, etc., follow in the same way, up to the plagal fourth mode).
At the same time, the Garrett 24 Palimpsest is also of special interest for
tracing the beginnings of Byzantine notation: see Figure 5.7.

## 6. Theta Notation

According to J. Raasted (1995, p. 57), Theta Notation is “a primitive notation that was
used for marking melismas in pieces that were otherwise simple, non-
melismatic. The marking was usually made with the letter Θ alone, or in combination with signs of
Coislin notation. (...) it was widely disseminated, in sources dating from the 8th to the 16th
century (or even later), and in use over an extended area, in Greek, Slavic, and Syro-
Melkite liturgical manuscripts.”xx
The same scholar hypothesizes that the roles of Theta Notation were:

 indicative, i.e. to serve as a guide for the hymnographers of kanons, who would write

prosomoia on the models of the heirmoi,

-  mnemonic, to remind chanters of the points where there is a melisma,

-  normative, allowing also a control of the performance by the person responsible for the chanting

(the hegoumenos or another cleric).xxi
In some cases, apart from Θ, other symbols also appear, such as circumflexes and
double or triple vertical strokes (British Library, Additional MS 24.378: Raasted, 1995, pp. 58-59).

## 7. Archaic musical scripts in Syro-Melkite manuscripts. The Theta and Double Notations

During the Byzantine period, the Syro-Melkites constituted a religious community which, unlike
the Monophysite Syrians, followed the Orthodox tradition of the emperor of the Eastern Roman
Empire. The word Melkite, etymologically, derives from the Semitic melek (ךלמ), which means
king.xxiii
Syro-Melkite liturgical manuscripts attracted the scholarly interest of various
Byzantine musicologists, such as Heinrich Husmann (1975, 1978), J. Raasted (1979), and others.
Apart from instances of the use of Theta Notation, sometimes in combination with double acutes and
double graves, Raasted also observed the existence of a related musical script, which he called
Double Notation, because of the predominance of the double acute (see Figure 5.10).
There is no lack, however, of points where the number of acutes multiplies noticeably, possibly in
some analogy with the density of the melisma, and where other signs also appear, such as the
grave and others: see Figures 5.11-12.

## 8. Cypro-Palestinian Notation

Cypro-Palestinian notation was the name given by I. Papathanasiou to a kind of primitive musical
script found in parchment liturgical fragments of the second half of the 12th and the first quarter of the
13th century, originating from the “broader area of Cyprus-Palestine” (Papathanasiou, 2003, p. 82).
Today these fragments are kept in the library of the Department of Music Studies of the
University of Athens, in the Kapadochos archive, nos. 1 and 2.
This local musical notation operates with the acute, the grave, and the
circumflex, which are placed in various combinations above the words of the hymnographic text:
see Figures 5.13-15.

On the basis of the above Sinaitic manuscripts L and MG 37, one may hypothesize that during the
last two centuries of the first Christian millennium, in the broader region of Palestine -
Egypt - Sinai, there were coordinated efforts to support musical memory, both for
emmelic recitation and for the melodic rendering of liturgical texts of Christian worship.
It seems that in this broader family of manuscripts of the 9th and 10th centuries from the aforementioned
region, a mnemonic notation is used, related to the “Cypro-
Palestinian” and “Double” notations, which we might perhaps conventionally call “Sinaitic”
(on the basis of the manuscripts in which it is found). It is based on the prosodic system and may
display various gradations of complexity, depending on the musical texture of the piece in question,
i.e.:

248

1. simpler, with words bearing double or triple accentuation, with the use of the acute, grave, and circumflex,

2. more complex, where double acutes, double graves, and sometimes triple acutes and others are also added. The root of the double and triple accentuations of words with the prosodic signs acute, grave, and

circumflex may probably be sought in the prehistory of the polytonic system.
According to the famous classical philologist Eduard Schwyzer (1990, vol. I, pp. 374-375), before the
establishment of the Byzantine system of polytonic accentuation known to this day, which is used
consistently only from the 9th and 10th centuries, but goes back to a reform by Theodosius
of Alexandria (around the year 400 AD), there existed the Alexandrian system, according to which
unaccented syllables could be rendered with the grave, while accented syllables with an acute or circumflex,
or even without an accent, leading in some cases to words with multiple prosodic signs: see Figure 5.22.
