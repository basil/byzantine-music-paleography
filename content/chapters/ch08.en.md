Summary
The present chapter deals with the modal system on which the musical setting of hymns and
troparia is based in the Byzantine and post-Byzantine periods. This is a system of eight modes, four authentic
and four plagal, which originated from the ancient Greek harmoniai (Dorian, Phrygian, etc.) and
underwent various systematizations over time, chiefly from Saint John of Damascus (8th c.)
onward. The theoretical foundations of the eight modes in Middle Byzantine notation are presented, together with their
visual and aural features, that is, their martyriai and intonations (apēchēmata), as well as various
Byzantine and post-Byzantine diagrams such as the Composite Wheel of Saint John Koukouzeles, the
Tree of Parallage, the Kanonion of the eight modes, and the kanons in psalterion form from the anonymous
theoretical handbook of codex 968 of the National Library of Greece. The chapter concludes with
various exercises concerning the identification of the initial pitch in stichera in all the modes,
as well as issues of rendering them on the staff.

## 1. Prolegomena
## 1.1. Definitional Approaches


Within the theory of the Psaltic Art, teaching on the modal system,i which is called the oktoechos (ὀκτώηχος) or oktaechia (ὀκταηχία),ii occupies a central place. Lists of apechemata (ἀπηχήματα: characteristic-identifying melodic formulas for each mode), exercises in parallage (παραλλαγή; a system of Byzantine solfège), a multitude of methodoi—didactic poems, a variety of diagrams, as well as various theoretical texts, present an exceptionally interesting modal system, with roots in ancient Greek music theory and a dynamic presence down to our own day.
According to the well-known music theorist of the first half of the fifteenth century, Gabriel Hieromonachos, the modes of the Psaltic Art are exactly eight, and everything that is chanted in church belongs to one of these eight modes. The eight modes are divided into two categories: four authentic and four plagal, deriving from the corresponding authentic modes. The modes bear a twofold designation: a. on the basis of their place of origin in Greece and Anatolia of Antiquity, and b. on the basis of their order in the system of the eight modes during Byzantine times: see Table 8.1 and Map 8.1.

Gabriel Hieromonachos emphasizes the distinctive profile of each mode, saying that “for the idea of each mode is like a certain color, which distinguishes each of the modes from the others.”v
In the Old Method, instruction in the modes included chiefly the martyriai of the modes, their various apechemata, the relationships among authentic, plagal (a fifth below the authentic), medial (a third below the authentic), and diphonic (a third above the plagal) modes, the many-branched chapter of parallage, as well as the subject of the so-called phthorai (signs indicating changes to another mode).vi
Developing the theory of the New Method, Chrysanthos (1832) defines the mode, among other things, in the following words:
“§ 281. In particular, according to musicians, a mode is a systematic scale, through which, proceeding in a determinate manner, they fashion the melody. That is, the mode is a scale of the systems, in which musicians proceed in a determinate manner, that is, beginning from specified notes, and dwelling on specified notes, preserving specified intervals as well, and ending on specified notes, they make the melody; and this determination of these things was made by the ancient musicians.
§ 282. A mode is an idea of melody, consisting in the habit of knowing which of the notes are to be omitted and which are to be taken up; and from which one must begin, and on which one must end. (...)
§ 301. The constituent elements of the modes are four: the Apechema, the Scale, the Dominant notes, and the Cadences. The identifying elements are two: the Apechema and the Cadences. For the mode is recognized immediately from the apechema, or from the cadence of some verse chanted beforehand.”


From a contemporary perspective, the Byzantine oktoechos constitutes a distinct manifestation of the broader phenomenon of modality. As a transcultural category, mode - modus comprises “concepts and phenomena of various musical cultures and periods, such as, for example, the harmoniai, the tonoi or modes of the ancient Greek world, the Byzantine modes, the modes of the Western Middle Ages (maneriae/toni-modi), the modes of the polyphonic music of the Renaissance (modi), the maggiore-minore, the makams of the Muslim world, the rāga of India, the modes of folk musical cultures, and many others.”vii It may be approached both as a modal structure, on an abstract, theoretical level, on the basis of the arrangement of notes and their functional relationships, and on an applied level, as dynamic pitch material in

# 1.2. Historical Evidence concerning the Byzantine Oktoechos


From various Byzantine manuscripts it is apparent that the musicians of the Eastern Roman Empire consciously associated the oktoechos with two great musical traditions of Antiquity: the ancient Greek and the Jewish.
The former is documented by the widespread use of ancient Greek names for the Byzantine modes; the latter arises from the central place of the Psalms of the Prophet-King David (10th c. BC) and of the other authors of the Psalter in the Byzantine services and, more generally, from the decisive significance of the Old Testament in the translation of the Seventy (Septuaginta) for Byzantine culture (see Table 8.1 and Figures 8.1-8.2).ix

During the ninth century, the Italo-Greek canonographer St Joseph the Hymnographer (812/818-886)
added to the collection of St John of Damascus corresponding offices for each day of the
week, creating the Great Oktoechos or Parakletike. The weekly cycle of the oktoechos
has been repeated from the eighth century to the present day. Each year it begins anew with the first
mode, on Thomas Sunday (the first Sunday after Pascha).
The famous Composite Wheel (Σύνθετος Τροχός) of St John Koukouzeles (ca. 1270-1340),xvii may be considered a second systematization of the eight modes; it will be presented in greater detail in

Next, a landmark in the history of the oktoechos is constituted by the canons of the Anonymous author of MS EBE 968,
which will be discussed in section 3.6.
Finally, a paradigm shift in the systematization of the oktoechos was brought about by the Three Teachers
Chrysanthos of Madytos, Archbishop of Dyrrachion (ca. 1770 - † 1843?), Chourmouzios Chartophylax (ca.

# 2. Theoretical foundations, martyriai, and apechemata of the eight modes in the Old System
## 2.1. Overall ambitus and the complete voice


In Byzantine Psaltic Art, the short pieces and, quite often, the moderately slow pieces as well, usually
exhibit an ambitus smaller than or equal to a heptaphonia (ἑπταφωνία; octave). In the slow chants, however,
and especially in the kalophonic ones, the ambitus may be considerably greater, reaching, in very rare
cases, the limits of what a very well-trained voice can do.
Explaining the first paragraph of the Protheoria of the Papadike, “The beginning, middle, end, and
system of all the signs is the ison; for without it the voice cannot be set aright” (see chap. 7,
Figures 7.6.a and 7.8), the anonymous theorist of manuscript EBE 968 (17th c.: Giannopoulos, 2013, p.


98) states the following:


“Know that each echos preserves one ison, and from that ison
are generated the oligon, the apostrophos, and all the ascending and descending voices.
And this ison is the first opening of the mouth, where
each person begins the tone of the echos. And the first echos, the one we call inside,
has the ison in the middle of the human voice; and a
person, if he has a fine voice, can chant a mathema (μάθημα) with seven ascending and
seven descending steps, and that voice is called complete.” (EBE 968, fol. 175v, in:
Giannopoulos, 2013, p. 110).


According to the aforementioned Anonymous, then, the characteristic feature of the complete voice is the
ambitus of two heptaphoniai: one above and one below a central ison–drone (ἰσοκράτημα) placed,
proportionally, in the middle of the human voice: see Figure 8.9.

# 2.2. Theoretical foundations of the Byzantine oktoechos according to the Old System


Through the thorough, combinatory study of Byzantine theoretical and musical manuscripts, and with the
aid of a very large number of transcriptions (from the beginning toward the end of the pieces and, if the
transcriptions did not work out, in reverse, from the end toward the beginning), foreign Byzantinomusicologists such as O.
Fleischer, the founders of the Monumenta Musicae Byzantinae, and their later collaborators and students
such as O. Strunk, J. Raasted, Chr. Thodberg, and others, delved into matters concerning the modal system
of the Psaltic Art as it appears in the Byzantine period.
We might say that the oktoechos according to the Old System has, at the theoretical level, a
“crystalline structure.” Below is an attempt to describe it, which also takes into account the
organization of pitch material in ancient Greek music theory (see Zannos, 1994, pp. 102-103, 175):
With the pitch Ke (a), which constitutes the root of the system, as the center, serving as the base of the first
authentic echos, the bases of the remaining authentic echoi are placed on the next melodic degrees of an
ascending triphonia (τριφωνία), in increasing order: echos 2nd with Zo΄ as its base, echos 3rd with Ni΄ as its base, and echos 4th
with the upper Pa΄ as its base.
Each authentic echos has its plagal counterpart a tetraphonia (τετραφωνία) lower. Consequently, the plagal of the first
echos has Pa as its base, the plagal of the second has Vou, the plagal of the third, or the so-called Barys, has Ga, and
finally, the plagal of the fourth has Di: see Figure 8.11.

It must be emphasized that all attempts at transcription, transnotation, or correspondence with
pitches of the New Method operate at the theoretical level and are conventional. Neither in the
New nor in the Old Method are absolute pitches involved. In practice, the chanter takes the pitch according to the
requirements of each piece, the capacities of his own voice or of the choir with which he is chanting, and
with his choice of pitch each time he enters into the broader context of liturgical practice.
Measurements of microintervals and clear descriptions of scale genera (diatonic, chromatic,
enharmonic) in the theory of the Psaltic Art appear from the Reform of the Three Teachers (1814-


15) onward. There are, nevertheless, very strong indications that the sound of the oktoechos in today’s


practice of the Psaltic Art is based on a tradition of many centuries, and that this tradition is at once dynamic
and stable, uniform in essence and diverse in its manifestations.
The use of the staff for the transnotation of Middle Byzantine notation does not imply an
equal-tempered conception of the old pieces. On the other hand, in transnotating pieces
from Middle Byzantine notation onto the staff, it is not customary to use differentiated
accidentals (e.g. reverse flat, single-stroke sharp, etc., as is often done in
modern transcriptions from the New Method), since measurements of the microintervals were made
later. Thus the issue of microintervals in the Old System remains open for
further research. The common and immediate basis, however, of both the Byzantine oktoechos and the eastern
maqamat, the Western medieval modi, and other modal systems, is the natural intervals,

# 2.3. Apechemata, martyriai, parallagai, and phthorai
## 2.3.1. Apechemata, modal signatures, parallage (I): Introductory remarks


Ēchēma, apēchēma, enēchēma, and proēchēma are related terms encountered in older and more recent theoretical writings to designate a characteristic melodic formula or a musical phrase that produces the distinctive sound of a mode (cf. Strunk, 1945/1970, p. 20. Raasted, 1966, pp. 1, 5. Floros, 1998, pp. 156-160. Troelsgård, 2011, p. 62. Chrysanthos, 1832, chap. 5. Karas, 1982, vol. I, pp. 229-230. Mavroeidis, 1999, pp. 99-100).
Among the related terms ēchēma and apēchēma, one may initially discern a subtle semantic differentiation:


1. ēchēma = the melodic formula or phrase that gives the pitch and the characteristic sound of the mode


(intonation formula),


2. apēchēma = specifically the variable ending of the melodic formula ('sounding-off', 'tail'), which


bridges the ēchēma with the beginning of the piece or phrase that is to be chanted (cf. Troelsgård, 2011, pp. 62-65, where reference is also made to the Latin system of differentiae in Western medieval chant, corresponding to the apechemata).
It seems that in the post-Byzantine period the term apēchēma prevailed over the term ēchēma and was generalized so as to designate, as a whole, the preparation of the mode by means of a characteristic formula or phrase.
It is in this more general sense that the term apēchēma will be used below.
According to J. Raasted, author of the important monograph on the subject of apechemata and modal signatures entitled Intonation formulas and modal signatures in Byzantine musical manuscripts (1966,


p. 160), the old apechemata could have various functions:
1. Facilitating the beginning of the melos that was to be chanted,
2. contributing to the embellishment of the performance and increasing the level of festivity,
3. coordinating the act of performance,
4. clarifying the musico-poetic structure of the piece.
Moreover, the apechemata occupied a central place in the theory and pedagogy of the Psaltic Art


under the Old System, since parallage was based on them.
As is well known, from 184-15 to the present, parallage has used the so-called
monosyllabic pitch names PA, VOU, GA, DI, KE, ZO΄, NI΄, which were created on the basis of the first
seven letters of the Greek alphabet, in combination with the five vowels /a/, /e/, /i/, /o/, /u/.xx
In the Old System, however, parallage was carried out with the so-called polysyllabic pitch names, which
formed small apechemata. According to the old conception of parallage, each pitch was, potentially, the basis of a mode. If the course of the melody was ascending, then it would be the basis of an authentic mode. Conversely, if the course was descending, then it would be the basis of a plagal mode. # 2.3.3. Relationships and subcategories of modes (I)


As was said in the previous sections, the core of the oktoechos consists of the four authentic modes, whose bases are placed on the four pitches of an ascending diatonic tetrachord: 1st mode: theoretical base a (Ke), 2nd mode: h (Zo΄), 3rd mode: c (Ni΄), 4th mode: d (Pa΄).
Four degrees below each authentic mode was the theoretical base of the corresponding plagal mode: plagal of the 1st mode: theoretical base D (Pa), plagal 2nd: E (Vou), barys: F (Ga), plagal 4th: G (Di).
Another important relationship that develops among the modes is that of mediality. According to the classical teaching of the Protheoria of the Papadike, which refers to the data of Middle Byzantine notation, a medial mode is each time placed two degrees below the high base of an authentic mode and will be a plagal mode:
“Take note that the medial mode of the 1st is the barys;
and the medial of the 2nd, the plagal 4th
and the medial of the third, the plagal 1st
and the medial of the 4th,xxiii the plagal 2nd” (Iviron 970, year 1686, fol. 5v, in: Stathis, 1997,


p. 44): see Figure 8.14.

![Description](/resources/image_10398.jpeg)

Figure 8.14. The relationship of mediality among the eight modes, according to the Protheoria of the Papadike.xxiv


The reverse relationship—that is, starting from a plagal mode and going two degrees above its base—leads to the so-called diphonic modes, which will be authentic modes. Thus the barys has the 1st mode as its diphonic mode, the plagal 4th the 2nd, the plagal 1st the 3rd, and the plagal 2nd the 4th mode. Some theoretical sources use the term “medial” by extension also for the diphonic modes.
A special case is the so-called legetos. Its martyria becomes known from the period of Kalophony onward: see Figure 8.15. Its base in the Old Method is Zo΄ (h), and its characteristic use is as a medial martyria in pieces belonging to the family of the 4th mode. # 2.3.5. Phthorai


Phthorai (alteration signs) are graphic symbols that indicate a change in the course of a melos, through the transition to a sound that belongs to another mode (or to another system or genus), during a piece.
In some cases (triphonic plagal 4th mode [α[α or triphonic plagal 2nd ]ε[α]ω) the phthora may also appear next to the initial martyria, especially from late Middle Byzantine notation onward.
In the New Method, the use of phthorai almost always presupposes a point of “binding,” where, with the appearance of the phthora, its operation begins, and a corresponding point of “release,” where a new phthora is placed, which either restores the original sound or indicates a further alteration (see, e.g., Giannelos, 2009, pp. 84-94).
In the Old System, the use of phthorai is much rarer, and the correct interpretation of phthorai is based chiefly on oral tradition. The Protheoriai usually present phthorai for most modes, either toward the end of the list of great signs or as a separate section (mainly from the 15th century onward: see chap. 7, Figure 7.6.c and Table 7.8).
The shapes of the phthorai do not remain the same in all Protheoriai. As shown in the following Table 8.7., alongside the most common shapes, most of which have been preserved down to the present within the framework of the New Method, there are also several other forms, either reflecting different scribal habits or due to copyists’ oversights.

# 2.3.6.2. Medial signatures (mesomartyriai)xxxi


In the musical manuscripts of the Byzantine period, medial signatures are placed for the most part at points
where there is musico-poetic punctuation in the hymnographic text (upper or middle points).
As to their role, medial signatures can
confirm the end of the preceding phrase, or
prepare the beginning of the following phrase, or
both (see Raasted, 1966, p. 73).

preceding phrase, and the second preparing the beginning of the next, in the event of a modulation:
cf. Troelsgård (2011, pp. 69–70).

# 3.6. The issue of chromaticism


The question of the existence or non-existence of chromatic modes in the Middle Ages in Gregorian chant and in Byzantine
music occupied many musicologists. With regard to the old Byzantine tradition, most
non-Greek scholars of the previous century adopted the view that the oktoechos was diatonic, since the
older diagrams, such as the Composite Wheel, the Kanonion of the Eight Modes, and the Tree of
parallage, all move according to the system of tetraphonia and are clearly diatonic in basis.
By contrast, many Greek researchers, such as S. Karas, Gr. Stathis, and others, emphasized the uninterrupted
tradition of chroma from Byzantine times down to the present.
Only from the 1980s onward did the view begin to gain wider acceptance also among
Western researchers that the family of the second mode (second, pl. second, and nenano) must already in Byzantine
times have exhibited chromatic intervals.xlvi
A strong piece of evidence for the existence of chroma already in the old, Byzantine,
classical repertoire is found in the use of martyriai (μαρτυρίες) within the musical manuscripts themselves.
More specifically, in pieces in the second or pl. second mode, cadences on the melodic degree located
two steps below the high base of the second mode, or two steps above the base of the pl. second (that is, on Di-
G), are usually indicated by a martyria of the second mode with two connecting apostrophoi above, whereas,
according to what the Wheel of parallage teaches, on the note Di-G there should appear a martyria
of the pl. fourth. The presence of the martyria of the second mode on the note Di-G indicates that the mode is structured with two
diphoniai which resemble one another, without being identical (small interval at the base, large at the
top): Vou-Ga-Di sharp, and Di sharp-Ke-Zo′ (cf. Zannos, 1994, pp. 103-122): see Figure 8.52.
Possibly this case of an apparent conflict between the martyria which ought
to be placed according to the Wheel as the pre-eminent system of the old parallage (pl. fourth) and that
which occurs within the piece (second), could be explained by adopting the conceptual pair “from
parallage” (ἀπὸ παραλλαγῆς) and “from melos” (ἀπὸ μέλους), which is used, for example, by Gabriel Hieromonachos when he speaks of
various kinds of metabole (ed. Hannick & Wolfram, 1985, MMB CSRM I, lines 565-575. See also Raasted,
1966, p. 26).

![Description](/resources/image_10488.jpeg)

Figure 8.52. Martyriai “from parallage” and “from melos” for the note Di: an apparent conflict.

# 3.8. Transposition and double parallage


In Middle Byzantine notation the phenomenon of transpositions is not rare, that is, the transfer
of a polychord to a melodic degree other than the usual one. This is a procedure which
has its analogue in the New Method, with metabole by tone, and also in Western European music,
with the so-called transporto.xlvii
In the New Method, in such cases, we perform pseudo-parallage, changing the names of the
notes according to the mode to which we wish to go. In tonal European music we can
change clef and key signature. In the Old System, at the time when theory defined the modes and
parallage chiefly by means of the system of the wheel, double parallagexlviii could be carried out at the points where
the usual sequence of the polysyllabic notes was annulled. An example from the famous
Method of the Sticherarion by Xenos Korones can clarify what is meant: see Figure 8.57.a.

# 4. A paradigm shift in the systematization of the oktoechos: The New Method


The Reform of the Three Teachers in 1814-15 brought significant changes at the level of the
systematization of the oktoechos, while at the same time attempting to preserve its traditional sonorities:

![Description](/resources/image_10497.png)

The manner of parallage changed: the polysyllabic notes α[α]ες, ]εα ]ες etc.
were replaced by the monosyllabic notes Pa, Vou, Ga, Di etc.


The theoretical bases of the modes were determined anew, without always preserving the
interval of a descending fifth between authentic and corresponding plagal modes.


The way the martyriai were written changed: the initial letters of the corresponding notes were placed on the martyria signs of the exegetical Middle Byzantine
notation.


Many of the old intonation formulas were set aside, and the use of a simple “]ε”
on the base of the mode at the beginning of pieces was proposed.


Modes and scales were classified into three genera: diatonic, chromatic, and enharmonic.


For the first time in the history of the oktoechos, measurements of microintervals were made in
moria (on the basis of the subdivision of the heptaphony-octave into 68 parts).lii


The dominant notes and cadences for each mode were also defined at the theoretical level


(see Chrysanthos, Great Theory, 1832).
The scales of the New Method are presented over the span of one heptaphony, although internally
they may follow various other systems of organizing the pitch material (tetraphonia-wheel,
triphonia or diphonia). The shape of the scales imitates the neck of the pandouris/the tambur, with
its perdes—an instrument widely disseminated in the practice and teaching of Eastern music
during the 18th and 19th centuries:liii cf. Figures 8.59-60.
