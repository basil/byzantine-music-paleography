## Summary

This chapter offers the reader a brief introduction to the world of Greek Palaeography and Codicology. First, it presents elements concerning writing materials (papyrus, parchment, paper), the forms of manuscripts and other features (scroll, codex, binding, ruling, palimpsests), the liturgical contexts of Byzantine musical codices, and elements for their analytical description. It then discusses the various types of Greek alphabetic script (majuscule and minuscule, with their subcategories), punctuation marks, as well as prosodic signs. The chapter concludes with aids for the reading and editing of Greek manuscript texts, and with sample exercises from manuscripts of the pre-Byzantine, Byzantine, and post-Byzantine periods.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

This chapter presupposes the study of Chapter 1. For further study in the field of the Palaeography of the Greek Language, reading the following books is recommended: Mioni (1998) and Litsas (2001).

## 1. Prolegomena

### 1.1. The Palaeography of the Greek Language and Its Branches: Definitions

The Palaeography of the Greek language is the science concerned with the evolution and the
formation of the Greek scripts, chiefly from Linear B (ca. 1550 BC) until the beginning of the 20th
century, when typography definitively prevailed in almost all fields of knowledge.i
Among the branches of Palaeography are the following:

● Epigraphy: Its object of research is inscriptions engraved on hard materials

(stone, etc.).

● Diplomatics: It deals with the reading and critical study of archival, public, and private

documents.ii

● Papyrology: It investigates papyri. This is a more recent branch of Palaeography, which

was created in the first half of the 20th century, after spectacular discoveries of papyri in Egypt (in cities
such as Oxyrhynchus) and in other parts of the Near East and the Mediterranean.iii

● Palaeography proper: It focuses on the study of manuscript codices from the 4th century AD until

the 19th and the beginning of the 20th century.iv
The “father of Greek Palaeography” (meaning the palaeography of the Greek language) is recognized as
the French Benedictine monk Bernard de Montfaucon (1655-1741), who is renowned for
the first handbook in this field, entitled Palaeografia graeca (Paris,1708)v (see Figure 2.1). In
this particular book, Montfaucon refers, among other things, also to Greek musical notation, which he
divides into two categories:

a) signs “for the movement of the voice in reading,” that is, for indicating melodic

recitation, a notation that was later called “ekphonetic,” and

b) signs “for greater and richer movements of the voice,” that is, for the notation of the

chants proper.vi

Figure 2.1. Bernard de Montfaucon (1655-1741), the father of the Palaeography of the Greek Language.vii
Source: <http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_de_Montfaucon> (13.07.2009).

Initially a chapter of Palaeography proper, Codicology is today a dynamically developing branch
of Palaeography. Codicology deals with the history and morphology of
codices (the Greek word κῶδιξ derives from the Latin caudex/codex, plur. codices = tree trunk,
piece of wood, book, writing).viii Specific issues of codicology include the external and
internal form of the codex (binding, writing material, type of script and scribe of the manuscript, etc.),
its dating, the patron who commissioned and financed the manuscript and its owners,
and in general the “journey” of the codex until it ended up in the particular library where it is kept
today, etc.: Habent sua fata libelli...ix

### 1.2. Writing Materials

Among the writing materials of Antiquity are numbered, among others, the following: papyrus, ostraca, wood,
stone, wax tablets, leather, cloth, metals, etc.x In the Middle Ages parchment first predominated and,
later, paper.
If we refer specifically to the written witnesses of Greek musical culture, these
are recorded on stone, clay, papyrus, parchment, paper and—from the end of the 20th century—on
digital media.
In the present book we shall deal somewhat more analytically with the materials that were used for
the notation of Greek music during the pre-Byzantine, Byzantine, and post-Byzantine periods, which
are, in chronological order, papyrus, parchment, and paper. A few words about
each of these follow below.

#### 1.2.1. Papyrusxi

Papyrus constitutes the basic writing material of Antiquity. The earliest examples of papyri
are documented in Egypt from the third millennium BC. Papyrus prevailed until the 4th century AD, when
it gradually began to be displaced by parchment. Its use during the Middle Ages is sporadic.xii
The word papyrus (in Latin cyperus papyrus) goes back to the Egyptian word pa-per-aa (= the things of
Pharaoh, royalxiii) and names a marsh plant which in Antiquity grew in Egypt
in the valley and Delta of the Nile, in Syria, in Mesopotamia, in Lake Gennesaret in
Palestine, and later also in Syracuse in Sicily, etc.xiv (see Figure 2.2).

In the Greek language papyrus was also called byblos/biblos, from the city of the same name in
Phoenicia (today in Lebanon), which was an important commercial centre for papyrus in Antiquity
(see Map 2.1).
All kinds of texts were recorded on papyri, from notes for everyday use, which constitute the
overwhelming majority of the surviving papyri, to major literary works and scientific texts
(mathematical, medical, music-theoretical, etc.). Most papyri are written in Greek and Latin
(ca. 80% of published papyri); the oldest are in Egyptian hieroglyphs, while still others are in Coptic,
Aramaic, or Persian.xvii
In the Mouseion of Alexandria, from the beginning of the 3rd c. BC, there operated the largest
library of the ancient world. It is thought to have held around 700,000 scrolls when it caught fire,
during the civil war in Egypt between Cleopatra and her brother Ptolemy XIII, in which Julius Caesar
intervened decisively, in the year 48 BC.xviii (see Map 2.1).

The rediscovery of papyri, which are very fragile and are preserved only in dry and enclosed places
that do not come into contact with moisture and air, began in the 18th century, with the excavations at
Herculaneum in Italy, where literary papyri were found that had been preserved in the lava of Vesuvius

campaign in Egypt (1798-1801) and gained great momentum in the late 19th and early 20th century, with the
excavations of the Egypt Exploration Fund of London (today the Egypt Exploration Society). Mention may be
made, indicatively, of the famous excavations by the classical philologists Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt
of Queen's College, Oxford, in the city of Oxyrhynchus (ca. 160 kilometres southwest of Cairo), between the
years 1897-1906, which brought to light enormous quantities of papyri in the Greek language.xx

Other important discoveries of papyri were made during the 20th century in the city of Dura-Europos
in Mesopotamia, on the banks of the Euphrates, and in the caves of Qumran (near the Dead Sea:
scrolls with biblical and liturgical texts, etc.).xxi
The oldest papyrus of Europe known today was found in 1962 at Derveni near

#### 1.2.2. Parchmentxxvi

By the name diphthera (διφθέρα)xxvii or membrane, from the 4th c. AD also as pergamene (sc. diphthera), or also leather (in
Latin: pergamena [neut. pl.] or, more commonly, membrana [sing., fem.], or even as vellum), there is known
a writing material made from the hide (flayed skin) of various animals, usually lamb or sheep, goat,
calf, more rarely ass (donkey) or even pig. The refined manufacturing method of
parchment includes the cleaning of the hide and its preparation as a smooth writing surface,
with the use of lime, water, and pumice.xxviii

According to Pliny the Elder (AD 23-79, author of Natural History - Naturalis
Historia, book XIII, ch. 21),xxix the Latin scholar Marcus Terentius Varro (116-27 BC) circulated a
story according to which the Egyptian king Ptolemy V Epiphanes (205-180 BC) and Eumenes II,
king of Pergamon (197-158 BC) of the Attalid dynasty, were competing as to who would
have the better library (in Alexandria or, respectively, in Pergamon). In order to halt
Eumenes’ work, the Egyptian king stopped the export of papyri to Pergamon, and thus
in the Asia Minor city, which at that time was at its zenith, the manufacture of
parchment began.xxx Although the reliability of this story was shaken by the fact that texts were found
written on a corresponding material already from the 4th c. BC,xxxi nevertheless this story reveals that
Pergamon was in Antiquity the principal productive and commercial centre of parchment.

During the 3rd-5th c. AD, testimonies concerning the spread of parchment become more numerous, and from the
6th century onward parchment becomes the most common writing material and predominates until the 13th century, when
it enters into competition with paper. In other words, parchment constitutes the basic writing material of
Byzantium.
Between the writing material (lamb’s hide) and the content of the New Testament there was created
in the Byzantine period a close symbolic association: see, indicatively, in the Revelation of John,
ch. 21, v. 27: “those written in the Lamb’s book of life.”xxxii
Luxury codices were made from high-quality parchment (e.g. the so-called
charta virginea, made from the skin of an unborn calf). Especially renowned are the so-called codices
purpurei—purple codices—of the golden age of St Justinian (6th c.) in the East, or of the age of the
Carolingians (8th-9th c.) in the West,xxxiii which consist of parchment leaves dyed purple,
on which the texts are written in gold and/or silver letters. Very often such
manuscripts are richly adorned with polychrome miniatures (see Figure 2.7).xxxiv

#### 1.2.3. Paper

Paper was invented in China, and the oldest samples of paper that have survived to the present
are dated around the year AD 100. It is made chiefly from linen, in combination with hemp and
cotton. The method of manufacturing paper (see Figure 2.8) remained secret until the middle of the 8th century,
when the Arabs of Baghdad learned it from captive Chinese papermakers.xxxvi

![Description](image_2453.jpeg)

Figure 2.8. The traditional process of paper production in China, based on several 17th-century drawings.
Sources: <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Making_Paper.gif> (8.8.2016). Gaur (1994, pp. 41-42).xxxvii

Subsequently, paper mills were founded in Samarkand, Aleppo, Damascus, and other cities
of the Middle East. This so-called Arab or Eastern paper also spread to Byzantium, especially from
the 12th century onward, and was used both in administration and for the production of codices,
gradually outstripping parchment. From the city of Bambyce in Syria, a commercial centre
of paper, there seems to derive the name bambykinon or bombykinon, which is used from time to time
for manuscripts made of paper.
With the Arabs, paper also came to the West: first to Spain, during the 12th century (paper mills
in Xàtiva of Valencia, in Córdoba, Granada, and Toledo), and a century later to
Italy. The first Italian paper mill was in the city of Fabriano and is attested from the year 1268. In
time it became the most important productive and commercial centre of paper in the West and East.

Arab or bombycine paper is characterised by its compact texture, its smooth and glossy
surface, its grey or yellowish colour, and its thick sheet, with dense laid lines and
freely distributed chain lines.
By contrast, Western paper has a rough surface, a white or yellowish colour, somewhat more widely spaced laid
lines, while the chain lines are distributed at regular intervals.xxxviii In addition, Western paper
usually contains the so-called watermarks, special designs (e.g. crowns, flowers, animals, etc.), which
constituted the identifying sign—the brand—of the particular paper mill.xxxix
The laid lines, the chain lines, and the watermarks are clearly visible when one holds the paper up to
the light of the sun or of a lamp: see Figure 2.9.

### 1.3. Writing instruments and inks

Writing on papyrus, parchment, and paper was done with black ink (with shades tending toward
brown and grey), which was called writing black (γραφικὸν μέλαν) or encaustum (λατ. encaustum), since it
was made over fire. The correct proportion of vegetable, later also tannic, substances and metallic
salts ensured the good quality of the ink, which, with the passage of time, would not fade
or, conversely, perforate the parchment or paper.xlii The use of red ink (sacrum encaustum)
in state documents was for a long period the privilege of the emperor (see Figure 2.11), and its
misuse was punished with severe penalties.xliii

In other categories of documents, however, no such restriction applied. In musical manuscripts, for example,

In red are written chiefly the titles and the so-called erythrogrammata (ἐρυθρογράμματα) (also called
rubrics, from the Latin ruber = red),xliv that is, notes by the scribe which concerned the

of the piece, and various liturgical instructions, occasionally also evaluations of the pieces, such as e.g. very

hypostases of cheironomia, chiefly from the period of Kalophonia (13th-15th c.) onward (see Figure

2.12). Finally, a variety of inks was in use for the miniatures or also for various diagrams, which

adorn luxurious Byzantine and post-Byzantine manuscripts, musical or otherwise.xlv

### 1.4. Forms of manuscripts, scribal workshops, and the preparation of a codex

#### 1.4.1. Forms of manuscripts: from the scroll to the codex

In classical Antiquity, the most widespread form of manuscripts was the scroll, i.e. the roll of papyrus,
more rarely also of parchment, wound around a rod of wood or bone, which at its ends
could have a covering/decoration, e.g. of ivory (see Figure 2.14).
Other names for the scroll were byblos, biblion, eiletarion (Lat. liber, libellus, volumen). The
papyrus scroll that had not yet been written upon was called chartes (Lat. charta). If a work was
written on several scrolls, because of its size, each scroll constituted a tome of
the particular work (e.g. volume I, II, III of the Iliad).xlviii

From the 2nd c. AD began the gradual replacement of the scroll and papyrus by the
codex and parchment. A codex is the term for a set of folded and sewn leaves, which
are fastened within a cover.xlix The name and form of the codex arose from
joined wooden waxed tablets (known by the names: pinax, pinakis, deltion,
deltidion, pyction, grammateion), which were in use in antiquity chiefly for everyday
writing, for teaching, etc.l Between wooden tablets papyrus
leaves could also be fastened, and later parchment leaves. The codex spread from the 4th c. AD,
chiefly in its parchment form, and prevailed in this form from the 5th-6th century onward. Of decisive importance for
the transition from the scroll and papyrus to the codex and parchment was the spread of
Christianity through Holy Scripture—the Bible (see Figure 2.15).li
From the 13th century onward, codices also appear in paper form.
Nevertheless, alongside the codex, the Byzantine tradition also retained the form of the scroll for
diplomatic documents (e.g. chrysobulls), for hymnographic texts such as the kontakion, or also for other
liturgical books, such as, for example, the various Divine Liturgies of the Byzantine rite.

#### 1.4.2. Scribal workshops

The copying-production of codices took place in large libraries (e.g. the imperial library in
Constantinople) or in large monasteries, where there were special scribal workshops, the so-called
calligrapheia (Lat. scriptorium, pl. scriptoria). Among the best-known calligrapheia that produced
manuscripts of Byzantine and/or post-Byzantine culture are those of the Holy Monastery of Stoudios and of the

Holy Monastery of the Panagia Hodegon in Constantinople (see Map 2.3), of the Archbishop of Caesarea

Arethas,lii of various monasteries of Mount Athos (e.g. Great Lavra, the Holy Monastery of Iviron, the Holy and Great Monastery of Vatopedi, the Holy Monastery of
Xeropotamou, etc.), of the Holy Monastery of Saint Catherine at Sinai, of various monasteries of Lower Italy, Crete,
Cyprus, the Danubian Principalities, etc.liii

#### 1.4.3. The preparation of a codex

Some of the basic steps in the preparation of a parchment or paper codex were the following:

√ The folding of the parchment into two or more parts (into four or into eight [Lat. in-quarto, in-octavo]

according to the size of the skin): see Diagrams 2.1-7).

√ The formation of the basic units of the book, the so-called quires, and the cutting of the leaves.

There are quires that consist of two bifolia (dyads or doublets, Lat. biniones), of three bifolia
(triads, terniones), of four bifolia (tetrads or quaternions, quaterniones), or even of more
bifolia sewn together. The most common form of quire is the quaternion, which
consists of 4 bifolia = 8 leaves = 16 pages (see Diagram 2.8).

To this day the sixteen-page quire constitutes the basic typographical unit. Each quire
received numbering by numeral letters on its first and last leaf (the so-called guards),
to ensure the subsequent correct assembly of the quires in the codex.lix

√ Ruling is the term for the preparation of the leaves with parallel and several vertical lines, which

were incised with a sharp instrument into the parchment, as an aid for the later alignment of the

### 1.5. Palimpsestslxvii

The making of books in Antiquity and the Middle Ages was an expensive undertaking. It suffices to
take into account that from one lamb, for example, only four manuscript leaves are produced; thus a more voluminous
codex of 400 leaves required skins from an entire flock! For reasons of economy, therefore, parchments
and more rarely papyri,lxviii could be recycled. Such recycled leaves are called
palimpsests (from the adverb palin + the verb psao [= I scrape off, erase], that is, leaves/manuscripts/codices scraped
again/for a second—and rarely for a third—time).lxix
The erasure of the earlier writing (underlying writing—scriptio inferior) included the
soaking of the parchment leaves in milk, their washing with a sponge, and their rubbing with
pumice.lxx After the leaves had dried, a new ruling could be carried out and the new text written
over it. From the process of rewriting, these codices are called in Latin
codices rescripti. Very often the new writing (overlying writing—scriptio superior) is
placed, for practical reasons, between the old lines, or in the opposite direction, since it
was not possible in all places to make the letters of the underlying writing disappear completely.lxxi
For the decipherment of the underlying writing, various aids have been used from time to time:
chemical reagents (e.g. tannic substances or ammonium sulphide), which, however, had
a destructive effect on the particular leaves, since after a short period of time they became
black,lxxii reading with an ultraviolet lamp, photography with infrared rays, etc.lxxiii
From the late 20th and early 21st century, new methods of studying
palimpsests were developed with the aid of computers, which led to spectacular results.lxxiv
The new methods for the study of palimpsests as well as of other difficult-to-read manuscripts
include advanced multispectral imaging techniques, Statistical Processing Techniques
applied to spectral images, etc. The various techniques are based on exploiting the difference between
the colour of the parchment, the first writing, and the second writing. Especially helpful in the
attempt to decipher the first layer of writing of a palimpsest are the processed,
pseudocolor images, which increase the difference between the two writings
and the parchment, rendering the various elements in colours that are more easily distinguished from one another
(see Figure 2.20).lxxv

### 1.6. Elements of Byzantine Codicology and the liturgical contexts of Byzantine musical codices

Modern Byzantine Codicology studies the dynamic process of the creation, establishment, development,
transformation, and dissemination of Byzantine manuscript codices (books) from the beginning of the 4th c. AD to
more recent times (19th-20th c.). Bearing in mind that liturgical life occupies a central position in the
Byzantine tradition, recent research aims at a holistic consideration-analysis of Byzantine
ecclesiastical-liturgical manuscripts within their generative worship context, on the basis of
codico-liturgical methodology.lxxix
The nucleus of the large family of Byzantine liturgical books consists of three
manuscripts with texts of Holy Scripture: the Gospel, which is the “heart” of the whole edifice
(containing readings from the four Gospels of the New Testament), the Apostolos (with readings
from the Acts of the Apostles and the Epistles of the New Testament), and the Psalter (it contains the
150 Psalms of the Old Testament) (see Diagram 2.1).

### 1.7. Analytical descriptive catalogues of manuscripts of Byzantine music (II)

Catalogues, and especially analytical catalogues of manuscripts of Byzantine music, constitute exceedingly important instrumenta studiorum for the palaeographical and, more generally, musicological study of musical
manuscripts. With regard to the cataloguing of codices of this kind, there are various models which
cataloguers have followed from time to time: general description, analytical description, and a mixed mode
of description. An indispensable guide to the process of analytical cataloguing of manuscripts of
Byzantine music was offered by Gregorios Stathis through his colossal cataloguing
work.lxxxiii Important models of analytical cataloguing of individual manuscripts are also found
in the accompanying Supplementa fascicles of the main Facsimilés series of the Monumenta Musicae
Byzantinae in Copenhagen.
An open list of elements that may be included in an analytical catalogue
of Byzantine musical manuscripts comprises the following:lxxxiv

1. Information concerning the type of manuscript according to its contents, its provenance, date, material, dimensions, number of folios, number of columns and lines per folio, palimpsest folios,

2. analytical description of the contents of the codex, with the incipits of all pieces and with bibliographical references for individual pieces (where these exist),

3. analysis of palimpsests (if any),

4. further comments concerning: • the codicological description of the manuscript: material of the manuscript, watermarks (for paper codices), quires and folios of the manuscript, ruling, size of the written frame per folio, pagination, etc. • palaeographical description of the text (type of script, handwriting and slant, characteristic forms of the script [special forms of certain letters],lxxxv punctuation, prosodic signs, abbreviations, type of script for headings and rubrics), inks, • description of the type of notation used in the codex, with mention of particular features (rare or idiosyncratic forms of certain signs, etc.), where these exist, • information on the scribe, • description of the ornamental elements and miniatures of the manuscript, • mention of important bibliographical notes by the scribe of the codex or by other hands, • description of the binding, • account of the state in which the codex is preserved, • data concerning the history of the codex, • observations by the author of the analytical catalogue concerning the structure, feasts (festal analysis), and importance of the manuscript, • up-to-date bibliography on the particular manuscript. #### 2.1.2. Continuous writing, punctuation marks, and prosodic signs

Although punctuation marks and prosodic signs had been introduced by Aristophanes of Byzantium, head
of the Library of Alexandria, already in the 2nd c. BC, the stable use of punctuation and prosodies was applied
only from the 8th and especially the 9th century onward, after Iconoclasm (726–843), in the general climate of renewal of the
Macedonian Renaissance (late 9th–first half of the 11th c.) and during the transition from majuscule to
minuscule script.
Majuscule script appears in manuscripts until the 8th/9th century, usually as
continuous writing (scriptio continua), that is, as a continuous flow of letters, without spaces between
words, without accents (acute, grave, circumflex) and breathings (smooth, rough), and without punctuation marks.
In some old manuscripts in majuscule script, these aids for reading the
text (punctuation marks, prosodic signs) were added later. Table 2.2 shows the

### 2.2. The majuscule script

Majuscule (Lat. maiuscula) is the name given to a script whose letters “are contained
between two parallel lines, without their strokes exceeding this double
limit.”xcvii
A great variety of forms of Greek majuscule script appears in written sources
dated from the beginnings of Greek alphabetic writing, the 8th c. BC, down to
the present. Tracing the developmental course of Greek majuscule script begins with the study
of inscriptions, where the letters are carved with a chisel in stone, first from right to left,
later boustrophedon (left to right the first line, right to left the second, again
left to right the third, etc.), and from the 5th c. BC from left to right.
From the 4th c. BC onward the use of the majuscule script of inscriptions (the
so-called epigraphic script) is also documented for the recording of literary texts on papyrus.
Until the 3rd c. AD the characters of Greek script were all majuscule. From the 4th
century onward various types of Greek minuscule script developed, first the so-called
cursive, and later also the bookhand.xcviii Table 2.4 lists some of the best-known types of

### 2.3. The minuscule scriptcix

Minuscule (Lat. minuscula) is the name given to a script whose letters are contained in an
interline space between two parallel lines, “but in addition two other parallel
lines are also required, one above and the other below, in order to enclose the strokes or other
features of the letters that extend beyond the central body.”cx
The appearance of Greek minuscule script is dated to the 4th century AD. As
mentioned above (see Table 2.1), minuscule script appears:

a. as cursive, with two branches: the Greco-Roman (4th–5th c.) and the Byzantine (from the 6th c. onward),

which, in turn, is documented in two forms, i. the common hand and ii. the bureaucratic script, and

b. as bookhand, which evolved from Byzantine cursive script, was standardized at the beginning of the 9th

century, and was used for the copying of literary and other texts.cxi

letters of minuscule Byzantine cursive script are distinguished, many of which also occur in
individual types of minuscule bookhand.

imperial or patriarchal auspices, go back the archetypal codices of almost the whole
literary heritage that has reached us from ancient Greece, and very little
would have been preserved from Greek literature had this vehicle not intervened,
which is a creation of the Byzantine Renaissance.”cxiii
Moreover, the transition from majuscule to minuscule script is considered “the most radical
reform” of Greek alphabetic writing in its almost three-thousand-year history. “The
minuscule, the fruit of a slow evolution over the course of centuries, was adopted for the
copying of religious and secular manuscripts, and, passing into
printing, still represents today the normal script of the Greek
language.”cxiv

xcvi In seeking the “method of liturgical reading” and, more generally, the tradition of reciting Greek
texts, A. Zografou (2010) observes, among other things, the following interesting points:

 “The use of punctuation marks differs substantially from manuscript to manuscript” (p. 214).

“The examination of the manuscripts shows that there was not, as also in the case of the ekphonetic signs, a
homogeneous notational system, but local traditions.” (p. 225).

 The punctuation of the original texts in the manuscripts constitutes an important source of information for the scholar/editor,

since “the liturgical utilitarian approach to the manuscript, that is, its oral performance, testimonies
of which are found encoded in a special semiology within the text itself” can
facilitate the understanding of the text, the detection of multiple readings, and the tracing of the
“living tradition of the text from generation to generation” (p. 214).

 A special system of punctuation for the loud reading of Greek texts was developed by the Alexandrian

grammarian Nicanor (2nd c. AD: see Hornblower & Spawforth [eds., 2003, p. 1041]). He uses eight types
of points: “full stop, lower stop, first upper stop, second upper stop, third upper stop, hypostigme
with enactment, hypostigme without enactment, separation.” This specialized terminology/notation was adopted by Basil the Least (mid
10th century) in his scholia on homilies of Saint Gregory the Theologian (p. 215) and leads to the conclusion that “the
loud reading of the orations of St Gregory the Theologian is situated in
continuation of the epic and theatrical tradition of ancient Greek literature; it constitutes
its substantial and living continuity” (p. 216). The system of Nicanor is attested in
various manuscripts until the end of the medieval period.

 On the basis of works by medieval commentators on ancient Greek texts, more ways of reading

a text can be distinguished:

1. “parenthetically” (concerns inserted text which is read with a different shade of the voice and

is indicated by the use of parallel acute accents at the beginning and end of the relevant passage),

1. “as one” or “in sequence” (concerns text that is read continuously, i.e. with each word pronounced in relation to

the preceding or following ones),

1. “in itself” (for separate reading of each word),

2. “with enactment” (with different coloration of the voice, aiming at the expression of specific

emotions. Double grave accents may be used [p. 224]).
Regarding the second mode of reading, A. Zografou observes that “Moreover, Byzantine musical
notation is based on the logic of succession, whereby the tonal value of each musical sign
is determined by the preceding one and determines the following one. Byzantine notation,
like ancient discourse (...) is based on a prosodic model of recitation; it is first
speech and then music” (p. 225 and with n. 20).

 Some other special symbols for the loud reading of texts are the following: “a point with a prick, a small

line below the last character of a word, small or large separations placed below,
in the middle or above the last character, and more rarely the apoderma "_| ". Also, the sign

of the large ‘hypodiastole’ resembles the ekphonetic signs of the apostrophos and hypokrisis”

(pp. 222-223). We thank Fr Gabriel Mândrilă for the article by A. Zografou.
