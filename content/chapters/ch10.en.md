## Summary


The present chapter continues the study of Middle Byzantine notation, with a deeper examination of
cheironomy as the traditional art of conducting a Byzantine choir, which reached its zenith during the
era of Kalophony (13th–15th c.). Through a historical approach, various milestones are presented
in the development of cheironomy, with the help of musico-iconographic and music-theoretical sources, especially those of
Gabriel the Hieromonk (first half of the 15th century). This is followed by a systematic approach to cheironomy and
then by various examples that make it possible to form a more concrete idea of the
various cheironomic patterns and their theological symbolism (M. Blemmydes and the Anonymous author of the ms.
of the Holy Monastery of Sinai 1764). The manuscripts Kastoria 8 and Konstamonitou 86 introduce us to the varied forms of the “hyperstaseis”
and great hypostases. Also discussed are the largely etymological interpretations of the
great signs of Middle Byzantine notation by Gabriel the Hieromonk. The study of the great
signs continues according to the tripartite scheme of form - name - musical meaning (Floros), with three
instrumenta studiorum:


a. A concise table of the graphic forms of the great signs, at all stages of Byzantine


notation, from Old Byzantine notation to the New Method,


b. a glossary of neume names, in which information from dictionaries concerning


etymology is gathered, along with multiple interpretations from various theoretical writings of the Byzantine, post-Byzantine, and modern
periods, and


c. an alphabetical catalogue of theseis (musical formulae) in Middle Byzantine notation, which in


the majority of cases are combined with great signs, taking as its central point of reference the didactic poem
“Mega Ison” by Saint John Koukouzeles, both in its original notation, from the celebrated ms. NLG
2458 of the year 1336, and from the slow exegesis by Chourmouzios Chartophylax.

## 1. Definitions and landmarks in the historical development of cheironomyi


Musical cheironomy (χειρονομία) may be defined as a “movement of the hand which regulates musical
performance.”ii
Forms of cheironomy are already documented from Antiquity in the Mediterranean basin, in countries
such as Egypt, Palestine, and Greece:iii see Figure 10.1.

In Byzantium, the cultivation of cheironomy is attested especially through historical,
iconographic, and liturgical sources. A striking literary source is the description of
psaltic activity in the holy churches of Hagia Sophia and Saint Demetrios, patron saint
of Thessaloniki, around the year 900, by John Kaminiates:
“for in each of these there had been appointed orders of priests, through whom mystical
worship is performed, and bodies of readers, through whom the hymnody of the chants
is pursued, crying aloud the verses antiphonally, and disposing the sound by the
cheironomies of the melodies, and forming a certain great and admirable choral array,
delighting the eyes of those who beheld them by the appearance of their glittering
vestments, and charming the ear by the artful lyre of the psalms.”v
The role of cheironomy in the invention of musical notation has not yet been fully
ascertained. Some signs, however, appear to have arisen from corresponding movements of the hand (e.g. the
petaste, the cross, and others).vi We may also suppose that cheironomy played an important role within
Palaeo-Byzantine notation, particularly in the melismatic genres of composition.
Nicholas Mesarites (1163/4–† 1220) speaks of the place of cheironomy in musical education,
vividly describing scenes from the schools at the Church of the Holy Apostles
in Constantinople:
“From there, looking westward, one would see [hymnodists] psaltodists with children
almost infants and still lisping, only recently taken from the breast, who nevertheless
open their mouths and speak wisdom and perfect praise to the king of all and God,
and to his saints who imitated his way of life and his sufferings. Passing on a little,
one would encounter youths with young men just emerging from boyhood, sending forth
from throat, mouth, tongue, lips, and teeth a well-rhythmed melody and concordant harmony.
They also move the hand for the equalization of voices and modes, guiding the novice,
as it were, so that he may not slip away from the high pitch, nor fall out of the
rhythm, nor deviate from concord and miss the melodious.” (Ηeisenberg, 1908, pp. 20-21, section 9. See also Wellesz, 1962,


1. 62-63, n. 3. Βuchwald, Hohlweg & Prinz, 1982, 521-522. Floros, 1998,


pp. 99-107).


A first definition of Byzantine musical cheironomy is found in Pseudo-
Damascene VII (mid–late fourteenth century):
“cheironomy is a rule handed down by the holy fathers, namely by Saint
Kosmas the Poet and Saint John of Damascus. For whenever the voice of the one about
to chant something goes forth, immediately so too does the cheironomy, so that
the cheironomy may show forth the melody.”vii
During the age of Kalophonia (late thirteenth–fifteenth century), cheironomy experienced
a great flourishing, as is documented by:


- the many great signs, “those of cheironomy,” in the musical manuscripts,

- the various methods for learning the signs of cheironomy (e.g. the Great Ison of Saint John Koukouzeles),

- the various interpretations concerning cheironomy in musical theoretical manuals (e.g. the treatise of Michael Blemydes),viii

- the very realistic depictions of psaltes during the Palaiologan period (e.g. in the Church of Saint Nicholas Orphanos, Upper Town of Thessaloniki): see Figure 10.2.

The most detailed description of cheironomy is owed to Gabriel Hieromonk (first half of the fifteenth
century). On this basis, we may conclude that cheironomy had the following roles:


1. Auxiliary: “we use [cheironomy] in the chants as a kind of helper and collaborator.


For just as those engaged in conversation seem to be at ease and become more fluent
when they move the hand—and some even their whole selves—so also psaltes will sing better
when moving the hand.”x


2. Deictic: Cheironomy is “that which distributes through the hand the proper positions to be made.”xi Nevertheless, it must be noted that


cheironomy cannot show metrophonia (the quantity of intervals):
“for since the apostrophos and the chamile are the same with regard to cheironomy,
but differ with regard to the voices—for the apostrophos has one


517




voice, whereas the chamile has four, while the three and the two descending voices
also have the cheironomy of the apostrophos—the scribe, looking to the one cheironomizing,
knows that he is indicating a descending voice or voices,
but he does not know how great the number is”:xii see
Figure 10.3.

![Description](/resources/image_10739.jpeg)

Figure 10.3. An illustration of the words of Gabriel Hieromonk (lines 706-712), concerning the
relationship between the bodies and the spirits: the former give their cheironomy also to the latter.


3. Coordinating: “if there were no cheironomy, there would be a multiplicity of voices but not


concord. For since we do not all sing different voices, but all the same ones,
it would happen that one would anticipate and another would follow,
and one would sing inside and another outside, if there were not something guiding all
to sing in concord. This is cheironomy; for by looking to the domestikos’ hand
we all sing in concord, and for this reason cheironomy is useful,
or rather most useful and most precious to us, only with dignity.”xiii


4. Determinative of musical orthography: “cheironomy distinguishes and examines these [i.e. the theseis],


whether they are correct or not. For since there are six signs having one voice each,
one would place them indiscriminately and not each in its proper place,
if there were not cheironomy, which makes known to us the place of each.”xiv


From all the above we may conclude that cheironomy was responsible for the artful
shaping of the chant and had to be taken into account in the notation of pieces. Gabriel
Hieromonk emphasizes that cheironomy is an indispensable element for the Psaltic Art:
“for it is not possible for anyone to utter anything in the psaltic art without cheironomy.”xv While the ison and the
bodies (the signs that indicate one ascending or one descending voice [interval of a second]) have
their own cheironomies, the spirits (elaphron, chamile, kentema, and hypsele) do not. Since, however,
the leaps too must be cheironomized, the spirits are combined with the bodies, so that “the signs
that possess cheironomy have imparted it to those that do not possess it, and in this way
all things can be cheironomized.”xvi
The Protheoria of the Papadike presents the great signs (abbreviation MgS) as the pre-eminent
signs of cheironomy, as is evident from the red headings of the corresponding catalogues
of signs: “these are the aphonic signs, those through cheironomy, which are called great
hypostaseis”xvii or, in later manuscripts, “the great aphonic signs, which are called
great hypostaseis, are these, set down by cheironomy alone and not by voice, for
they are aphonic.”xviii
Gabriel Hieromonk also informs us about the general role of the MgS: as the prosodic
elements in grammar, so too the MgS in the Psaltic Art serve for orientation within


518




the musical text and for its shaping, indicating rhythm or tempo, dynamics, and other details
of performance:
“Since we need not only the twenty-four letters, but also require the ten prosodies
for the euphony of speech, so too not only are the fifteen vocal signs useful,
but also the thirty-six aphonic signs, which also have the role of prosodies
in the psaltic art. For these are, as it were, a guide and leader as to whether to sing
in this or that way, or to handle the voices slowly or quickly,
with intensity or quietly. For the vocal signs make the voices, as we have said,
whereas the delays and quickenings and the other forms of the melodies are made by these
great signs.”xix
At the same time, the MgS remind the choir leader of the appropriate movement of the hand:
“For behold, we place an oligon and then an oxeia, and after these three
apostrophoi, and we ascend the two voices of the oligon and the
oxeia, and the three descending voices of the three apostrophoi. Yet we do not
know how these must be sung, whether slowly or quickly, unless the gorgon
or the argon is placed above; nor yet how the voice must be shaped or how
this thesis must be cheironomized, unless we also write the tromikon beneath.
And for these reasons the great signs are useful”:xx see Figure 10.4.


Figure 10.4. An illustration of the words of Gabriel Hieromonk (lines 251-258), concerning the relationship between the
emphonic and aphonic signs, with the thesis of the tromikon as an example.


During the period of the new embellishment (ca. sixteenth/seventeenth–eighteenth c.), the number of MgS increases
dramatically in musical manuscripts. This phenomenon may be interpreted as the notation of
cheironomy, for the preservation of the tradition, in a period during which the high art of directing
psaltic choirs in Byzantine times was gradually falling into oblivion. On the other hand, the decline of
cheironomy is connected with the appearance of written exegesis.xxi
A final testimony concerning the use of artful cheironomy in the Psaltic Art belongs to
the Dominican monk Jacques Goar (1601-1653):xxii
“The word ‘cheironomizing’ does not simply mean raising and lowering the
hand according to the custom of changing the measure (ad numerum manus modulandi
moram) and determining the order. On the contrary, the Greeks rarely chant at the
lectern (he means from books). The kanonarches recites the clauses of the troparia from the
book, and before everyone the director of the choir shows, by various movements and
gestures of his right hand, with fingers raised, lowered, extended,
contracted, or joined, instead of musical characters, the various shapes of the
chant and the turns of the voices. Thus all the others look attentively to this leader
of the chant, following the rule of the whole performance (totius modulationis regulam).”xxiii
In the first half of the eighteenth century, Kyrillos Marmarinos reports that cheironomy was by then
deprived of a theoretical basis and was performed only practically.xxiv With regard to the MgS he observes that
“They too have their correct effect when they agree with the lines;
since it is often possible to see a different line and different

Also, comparing the cheironomy of Byzantine and Ottoman music with each other,


Marmarinos clarifies that “that cheironomy among us was a movement of the hands shaping the
melody; but their (i.e. the Ottomans’) cheironomy is the rhythm and measure of the melodies.”xxvii
Basileios Stephanides emphasizes in his theoretical treatise (year 1819) that cheironomy
concerned not only meter, but also ornaments and dynamics of the voice:
“And cheironomy in the elementary teaching itself is considered not only as
a measure of time, that is, the hand ascending and descending, but
also more properly as providing the notes with a certain sharpness or
intensification; hence, according to the varieties of its shapes, the signs
that indicate in the lines or theseis the various modes and formations of this cheironomy
receive different names.”xxviii
According to the same theorist, the musical content of the MgS and cheironomy are taught
only orally:
“The meaning and cheironomy of these signs is not taught in writing,
just as neither are the modes and the figures of the rhetors, which are
suited to moving the passions, but only through the living voice and the perceptible
movements of the teacher.”xxix

# 2. A systematic approach to cheironomy


Considering cheironomy from a systematic perspective, we observe that the following elements are distinguished:


1. The shape of the hand, which conveys a specific musical message,xxxvi

2. the graphic representation: great signs, bodies, ison,

3. the musical result, the musical shaping. Musical shaping (Ger. Gestaltung) is realized in the domains:


α. of time,


1. of intervallic space and

2. of expression.


Consequently, musical shaping is realized in the following parameters of musical performance:


1. rhythm and tempo,

2. embellishments of various levels and modulations,

3. dynamics, articulation, timbre, phrasing, ethos. Some basic questions that arise in the study of cheironomy are the following:  What were the specific shapes of the hand?  When were these shapes used?  What was the relationship between the shape of the hand and the graphic shape of a sign?  In what does the musical result consist at which the shapes of the hand and the graphic signs of cheironomy (especially the great signs) were aimed?
## 3. Examples of music-theoretical, iconographic, practical, and comparative sources for the study of cheironomy


Research into the important chapter of Palaeography that is called “cheironomy” (χειρονομία) is based on the
combined study of various types of sources:


- music-theoretical: lists of signs, didactic poems, texts referring to cheironomy,

- practical: musical manuscripts,

- iconographic: depictions of chanters in wall-paintings and icons, especially of the Palaiologan period,

- written: references to cheironomy in ecclesiastical literature, in historical texts, etc.,

- comparative: cheironomy in other musics, cheironomy in sign language, etc.


In the brief historical overview of cheironomy with which the present chapter began, representative
passages have already been cited from various music-theoretical writings in which the subject of cheironomy
is also touched upon.
Whereas in most theoretical manuals cheironomy is taught less by words and
more through lists of signs and didactic poems (e.g. the Mega Ison), in the interpretation of the
signs by Michael Blemydes (prob. 13th c. [Uspensky]), cheironomy constitutes the author’s main subject
and is developed in the form of questions and answers, with a descriptive-symbolic character, as
may be seen from the following passages:
“Beginning, with God, of the signs interpreted one by one; composed
by the most learned kyr Michael Blemydes.
Question: In the type of what is the ison cheironomized?
Answer: In the type of the Holy Trinity; just as the Holy Trinity is, the
three one; and neither is the Father greater according to the essence of the divinity, nor the
Son, nor the Holy Spirit; so too the ison is sung, with
the fingers also brought together. (...)
Question: The oxeia is cheironomized in the type of what?
Answer: In the type of sharp spears, or so as to imitate the sharp nails.”xxxvii

# 4. “Hyperstaseis” and “great hypostases of cheironomy.” Interpretations and tables of great signs


Perhaps the most impressive Byzantine musical manuscript for the art of cheironomy is the Asmatikon
of the Holy Metropolis of Kastoria, no. 8, of the 14th century, in which two series of musical
notation may be distinguished: the first, above the poetic text, which is the familiar Middle Byzantine
notation, and the second, with large-form signs in red ink (in a few cases also in
green).xliv These signs, known in the musicological literature as “hyperstaseis,” concern
entire musical formulae/theses and are probably correlated with the gestures of the
domestikos/choir-leader. As K. Floros observed, they display kinship with the signs of
Palaeo-Byzantine notation of the Athonite type, as well as with the Old Slavonic kontakarion
notation, which has its roots in Palaeo-Byzantine Athonite notation:xlv Figure 10.10.

# 5. The role of the great signs and levels of stenography in the Old System (A). Musical theses


We saw in the preceding table that some signs are present from Palaeo-Byzantine notation
to the present, while most are abolished within the framework of the New Method. The evolutionary stage at
which we can identify the greatest number of great signs is that of late
Middle Byzantine script. The early stages of both Palaeo-Byzantine and Middle Byzantine notation, but
also the exegetical script, use a more limited number of great signs. These
details can be observed on the basis of systematic study of the musical codices of the
corresponding periods.
It must be emphasized that the presence of a sign in all the evolutionary stages of
notation does not automatically entail the preservation of exactly the same meaning of the sign
throughout the centuries. In other words, a graphic shape may indeed be present from Palaeo-Byzantine
notation to the present, but its musical meaning may have been differentiated (e.g. contracted,
expanded or transformed) over time.
On the basis of the comparative study of Palaeo-Byzantine and Middle Byzantine notation (from early
to late), Constantine Floros established that:
“Most researchers interpreted the hypostases as expressive or auxiliary
signs. By contrast, from my own research it emerged that the so-called aphona
are for the most part Palaeo-Byzantine signs which initially
served as stenographic symbols for notating two-tone or many-tone
figures and later, in the process of the evolutionary course of the
notations, were analysed into individual signs. Thus many of them lost
their primary meaning and gradually were transformed into expressive
signs. In Middle Byzantine notation some retained their function
as a kind of foundation or support, while others were abolished because
they were considered superfluous.
The following observation is essential, namely that Middle Byzantine notation, while
using very few great hypostases at the beginning of its evolution, by contrast
uses very many at its end. Middle Byzantine neumations of the 13th
century contain, as a rule, a very small number of hypostases. In notations
from manuscripts of the 14th century we observe, by contrast, a larger number
of hypostases and, finally, in the late Byzantine notations of the 15th century
so many appear in red ink that the reader might think that
they constitute the principal notational body.” (Floros, 1998, pp. 152-153).lii


In other words, it is observed that the great signs in Palaeo-Byzantine, adiastematic
notation have a high level of stenography, implying various musical theses, which
contain both their basic intervals and their musical shaping, that is, the rhythm, the
ornaments, the expression and the cheironomic shape of the particular thesis.
In Middle Byzantine notation, the level of stenography is partly lowered and the
notation becomes diastematic. Consequently, the great sign becomes aphonon, that is, it loses the property
of implying also the basic intervals of the formula. These are now supplied above, around or
also below the great sign, with emphona signs, which show the structural pitches of the
particular thesis. The aphonon great sign becomes above all a great hypostasis of cheironomy,
reminding the chanter of the musical shaping or execution of the particular thesis and its
cheironomic shape: see Table 10.4.

# 6. The Mega Ison of Saint John Koukouzeles. Levels of stenography in the Old System (B)


The didactic poem par excellence of the theses of the old and kalophonic repertoire in late
Middle Byzantine notation is the so-called Mega Ison of Saint John Koukouzeles (ca.
1270-before 1340),lvii the foremost representative of Byzantine Kalophonia.
It contains sixty-five theses, which are chanted with their own names as text. This method
was recognized by scholars as a golden key for the palaeographical study of Palaeo-Byzantine
and Middle Byzantine notation and, at the same time, for the musicological analysis of the old and
kalophonic repertoire, since it permits the direct recognition of theses and hence the understanding
of the structure of pieces at the microsyntactic level.lviii
Scholars such as K. Psachos (1978, pl. 19: collation of the poem in late Middle Byzantine and
exegetical script, with the exegesis in the New Method), O. Strunk (manuscript collations of old
sources),lix G. Dévai (1955: edition), E. Tončeva (1974: comparative palaeographical study), C. Floros (1970,


vol. III: use of theses for the study of Palaeo-Byzantine and Old Slavonic notation), Gr. Stathis


(1997: commentary on the repertoire of theses in relation to the old Sticherarion), Chr. Troelsgård (1997: comparison
with the Mega Ison of John Glykys), B. Karastoyanov (2007: musicological analysis), Fr Spyridon
Antoniou (2008, pp. 330-363: table with collation of sources and identification of the theses, for teaching
purposes), and others have dealt with this poem, and the series of scholars continues down to the present.
