## Summary

This chapter offers an introduction to the study of ekphonetic notation (εκφωνητική σημειογραφία), which is
documented chiefly in manuscripts of the 9th–15th centuries and concerns “Byzantine recitative,” i.e. the melodic
reading of passages from the New and Old Testaments. After a brief presentation of the various
types of sources with ekphonetic notation (Gospel, Apostle, Prophets) and reference to hypotheses
concerning the origin and antiquity of ekphonetic practice and the corresponding notation, the
reader’s attention is directed to the Byzantine lists—didactic poems—of ekphonetic
notation. On their basis we are informed specifically about the crystallized, classical system of
ekphonetic writing (11th–12th c.), which constitutes a mnemonic system that operates with 15 pairs
of signs, which are placed each time at the beginning and end of kola (musico-rhetorical units),
guiding the melodic reading of the sacred text according to specific formulas, known on the basis of the
oral tradition of the time. In addition, a case study by Sandra Martani is presented, concerning
the ekphonetic notation in the Vienna Gospel suppl. gr. 128, which is exceptionally rich in combinations.
The chapter concludes with a presentation of the project of critical editions of the Prophetologion, by
Carsten Høeg, S. Engberg, and other collaborators of the Monumenta Musicae Byzantinae, as well as with
reference to various attempts to decipher ekphonetic notation, on the basis of the
manuscript, theoretical, and contemporary oral tradition of ekphonesis.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

The study of Chapters 2 and 3 is required. The study of the article is also recommended: Martani, Sandra.
(2003). The theory and practice of ekphonetic notation: the manuscript Sinait. gr. 213. Plainsong and
Medieval Music, 12, 15-42.

## 1.2. Definitions

The term ekphōnēsis (ἐκφώνησις, from the verb ἐκφωνέω) generally designates an act of public and loud
recitation. In the context of the services of the Byzantine rite, it is combined with the intoned recitation (Lat.
lectio sollemnis) of pericopes from Holy Scripture, of the kontakion, and of various prayers and petitions. It constitutes
a kind of liturgical recitative, a speech-like chant (Ger. Sprechgesang).vii
As ekphonetic notation there is known today in Byzantine musicology a special type
of Greek notation, which regulates the intoned recitation of scriptural texts. It uses
for the most part pairs of signs, which are placed above, below, or between the lines
of the scriptural text, dividing it into short musico-rhetorical phrases (the so-called cola [κώλα]), which,
in turn, are assembled into larger units, the periods.viii The ekphonetic
signs are usually written in red ink, though there are also cases in which they are
written in the same ink as the scriptural text (dark brown or black), especially in the older
MSS.ix

## 1.3. Sources of ekphonetic notation, chronological limits, periodization

The study of ekphonetic notation is based chiefly on the following kinds of sources:

- Practical sources: liturgical books with ekphonetic notation,

- theoretical sources: Byzantine lists–didactic poems with ekphonetic signs,

- the present-day oral practice of ekphōnēsis in services of the Byzantine rite,

- comparative sources (e.g. the Hebrew tradition of the ekphōnēsis of texts of the Old Testament, Latin ekphonetic notation, etc.).x

Regarding the first category of sources, it should be clarified that ekphonetic notation
is documented chiefly in manuscripts of the 9th–14th centuries, of the following three types:

1. Gospel Books,

2. Apostoloi and

3. Prophets.

The sources of this period indicate an evolutionary course of the notation, which was divided by

1. Archaic or pre-classical system: in MSS of the 9th–10th c.,

2. classical system: known from MSS of the 11th–12th c.,

3. declining system: chiefly in MSS of the 13th–14th c.xviii

The classical system is regarded as the “zenith” of ekphonetic notation, to which
usually also the lists of ekphonetic signs, which are contained in various codices, such as, for example,
Leimonos 38 and others.xxi Significant examples from various manuscripts with ekphonetic notation of the
archaic and classical systems (9th–12th c.) are found in Thibaut (1913, pp. 34-51).
On the other hand, ekphonetic signs in later manuscripts (15th century and
isolated cases up to the 17th century) document the disintegration of this written
musical system, which led gradually, already from the 13th century, to the dissolution of the
aforementioned pairs, to the reduction in the number of signs, to their purely graphic-
decorative presence, and finally to their disappearance from liturgical books.xxii

## 1.4. Hypotheses on the origin of Byzantine ekphonesis and ekphonetic notation

The strong convergence shown by the ekphonetic signs (sēmadophōna) at the graphic and terminological
level with the prosodic signs of the ancient Greek language makes evident the dependence of this
written musical system on the prosodic system and, indirectly, on “the actual musicality of the
Greek language.”xxiii In addition, it appears that the various punctuation systems of ancient Greek
grammar also constituted a point of reference in the formation of ekphonetic notation.xxiv
It has already been mentioned that C. Høeg argues in favor of an early genesis of the ekphonetic
parasemantic notation (4th c. AD). S. Engberg prefers, on the basis of the surviving sources, to place the
creation of ekphonetic notation in the 9th century, after Iconoclasm, in the broader
context of the Macedonian Renaissance. That is, at the same time as the transition
from majuscule to minuscule script takes place and the systematic use of prosodic
signs begins: see, e.g., the Uspenskij Tetraevangelion of the year 835 (photographs in ch. 2, Assessment
criterion 2.6), where one can observe on the first folio a kind of proto-ekphonetic
notation, in combination with prosodic signs and various punctuation marks.
Furthermore, S. Engberg connects the formation of the ekphonetic system with the important
missionary activities of the Byzantines during the 9th and 10th centuries, seeing in this notation an
effective tool for disseminating the Greek manner of reciting the Holy Scriptures also among other
peoples.xxv
Regarding the origin of Byzantine ekphonetic practice, various scholars consider that
the following are possible sources:

α. On the one hand, the traditions of ancient Greek rhetoric, epic, and theatre, with which

both the ekphonetic and the reading tradition in Byzantium are related.xxvi

β. On the other hand, the reading of Old Testament texts by the Jews of the Diaspora,

during the Hellenistic period, on the basis of the Septuagint Translation.xxvii
From the creative combination of these elements, it appears that Byzantine cantillated
recitation was born.

## 1.5. Methodology for the study of ekphonetic notation

As mentioned above in section 1.3, study in the field of ekphonetic notation is based
today on the combined analysis of data from:

- Liturgical books with ekphonetic notation (practical sources),

- lists-didactic poems with ekphonetic signs (a broader category of music-theoretical sources),

- the oral practice of ekphonesis today and on the basis of older recordings of readings by representative bearers of the ekphonetic tradition.

In addition, research into Byzantine ekphonetic notation is broadened through
interdisciplinary comparisons with related traditions (e.g. Hebrew, Latin, Coptic, and others).
Above all, however, the study of ekphonetic notation and of its uninterrupted oral
tradition gains depth when it is considered within the space that generated it, the space of Orthodox
worship, as it was formed in Byzantium and evolved down to the present day. Sandra Martani emphasizes:

"Only an integrated consideration of both music and liturgy permits a genuine understanding of
the totality and complexity of Byzantine worship. If such an interdisciplinary approach is
deemed important for the study of Byzantine hymnography, one can understand how essential it
is for the study of 'ekphonesis', the system used for the cantillation of Sacred Scripture within
the liturgy. In this context the interpenetration of artistic form and divine revelation is even
more intimate. Unfortunately, since the Byzantine ekphonetic notation cannot now be
deciphered, we are denied a full understanding of its richness. Nevertheless, a meticulous study
of the medieval system for the cantillation of the Scripture can potentially foster a better
understanding of its unique characteristics and the role that ekphonesis played in the
proclamation of the Scriptures in the Orthodox Church."xxviii

The study of ekphonetic notation comprises various levels, each with particular
working steps. At a first, basic level of study, the following can be done:

- Learning the ekphonetic signs, through the relevant Byzantine didactic lists and modern systematizations,

- carrying out exercises in ekphonetic notation, on the basis of codices in majuscule and minuscule script, starting from the classical system. The exercises include the transcription of the text with all additional signs (prosodic, punctuation, ekphonetic), and the identification of the musical signs.

- Listening and practice in cantillated recitation according to present-day practice.

The study can continue at a second, more advanced level, with the carrying out of the following
working steps:

- Exercises with the pre-classical and the declining system,

- study of critical editions and an attempt to prepare a small edition of a pericope, on the basis of various sources, with the preparation of the relevant apparatuses,

- study of the specialized bibliography on ekphonetic notation,

- deeper engagement with the present-day practice of cantillated recitation,

- carrying out comparative studies between Greek cantillated recitation and other related traditions.

The following sections aim to facilitate the basic study of ekphonetic notation
and to prepare the ground for the more specialized study of this notation, for those who
may later wish to proceed toward this field.

## 2. The signs of ekphonetic notation

## 2.1. The Byzantine Lists of Ekphonetic Sign-Formulas

The earliest Byzantine theoretical texts concerning the Psaltic Art consist of lists
of a mnemonic-didactic character, containing names and shapes of signs—corresponding melodic
formulas.
For ekphonetic notation the following six lists are known today:

- Sinai 213, fol. 116v, 10th cent.? (Harlfinger/Reinsch/Sonderkamp/Prato), without a heading. This is the earliest list of Byzantine neumes.

- Sinai 217, fol. 2r, although later than the preceding list, can nevertheless be compared with it in the number and arrangement of the signs (Martani) / 14th cent. (Høeg). The list bears the heading: «† The beginning, with God, of the tones of the Apostles and of the Gospels (sic), thus †».

- Sinai 8, fol. 303r, late 12th cent. (Høeg)/ca. late 11th cent. (Floros), without a heading. This is a didactic poem, in which the formulas of ekphonetic notation were also transcribed into Old Byzantine notation. The order of the formulas differs from that in all the other lists.

- Leimonos 38, fol. 317v, late 12th cent.? (Høeg), 12th cent. (Engberg), without a heading.

- Tbilisi Q 908, fol. 279r, 13th cent.? (Εngberg). This is a Georgian manuscript. The list of ekphonetic sign-formulas is written in Greek, but the heading is in Georgian and has the following content: «Signs and sounds of the four Gospels and of the Apostles, according to the Greek melody».xxix The lists of the Holy Monastery of Leimonos and of Tbilisi are the most complete, representing the fully developed ekphonetic notation of the classical period (11th-12th cent.). They function in a sense as «didactic pericopes», since they preserve the structure of actual scriptural readings.

- Finally, there is an additional MS, known today only from the descriptions of I. Tzetzes, probably of the 12th cent. (Engberg). xxx

## 2.3. The principal characteristics of the pre-classical and classical system

It has already been mentioned that the pre-classical system of ekphonetic notation appears in Gospel Books, Apostolos lectionaries, and Prophetologia of the 9th–10th centuries, while the great flourishing of this system of notation is observed in corresponding codices of the 11th–12th centuries, with the classical system. More specifically,
it is considered that, during the 12th century, ekphonetic notation reached the highest point of its
development.xlviii The following Table 4.4, which is based on clarifications by S. Martani, brings together basic
features of the two systems, starting from the crystallised form of the classical
system:

|Classical system|Preclassical system|
|---|---|
| It operates with 15 pairs of signs, of which: - four have the teleia (τελεία) as their second sign and are placed at the end of a period of the biblical text. - The remaining pairs appear within a period. They are homogeneous, that is, they consist of the same sign at the beginning and at the end of the musico-rhetorical unit. The so-called apeso exo (ἀπέσω ἔξω) (apostrophos-oxeia) is an exception.  The use of pairs with synemba (σύνεμβα) and teleia, as well as with syrmatike (συρματική) and teleia, is frequent. ## 3. Critical editions of liturgical texts with ekphonetic notation

In contrast to the printed book, which is produced in a series of identical copies, the various manuscript
copies of a text always present certain—smaller or larger—differences among themselves.
Comparisons among various manuscripts of the Holy Scriptures began in the early Christian
period and continued during the Byzantine centuries. Later, in the age of Humanism, classical
philologists and theologians began wide-ranging comparative studies of manuscripts, with the aim of
creating critical editions of Greek and Latin texts. From the nineteenth century onward, similar studies began to
be undertaken for Gregorian chant (the school of the monks of Solesmes), and from the twentieth century
also for Byzantine music.lv
In general, the undertaking of a critical edition of a text rests on the collection of the relevant
manuscripts, the determination of their mutual relationships, and their selection and grouping through the
creation of a special diagram, the so-called stemma codicum. The editor’s basic tool
is the collation of sources (comparative study of the manuscripts), and the selection of the best codex

(the oldest/the most complete/by a reliable copyist) as the guide (Lat. dux) for the reconstruction of the

original form of the particular text. This procedure (known as emendatio in Latin, from the
verb emendo = I remove errors, I perfect), requires deep study and complex processes of judgment
on the part of the editor (corrections, supplements, etc.).lvi The collation of sources also permits the
preparation of the critical apparatus, in which the various variants are condensed (all of them or a selection,
at the editor’s discretion) from the manuscript tradition of the text, and possibly also various other
proposals for correction on the part of other editors. The language used in compiling
critical apparatuses is, in the overwhelming majority of cases, Latin, thus continuing the tradition of the
Humanists.lvii
A special case, however, is presented by the undertaking of critical editions of Byzantine liturgical
musico-poetic texts, which proves particularly multifaceted, not only because it involves a double
text (words + music), but also because the texts usually have both a very rich and
long-lasting written tradition and a dynamic oral presence within the worship life of the
Church. Thus many and multi-layered variants arise in the texts and especially in the musical
notation. Consequently, the creation of a stemma codicum is often impossible (because of the
large number of sources and the volume and variety of the variants). Earlier musical editions
attempted to present a critical text consisting of elements taken from various
manuscripts, which would in each case reflect the majority of the sources. During the twentieth
century it became clear, however, that the principal aim of the editor of a Byzantine musico-poetic text could
not be the reconstruction of its archetypal form, since this is usually lost in the depths of
time, and the assembly of heterogeneous elements from later manuscripts with various differences
among them leads only to a text without historical substance. By contrast, a critical edition
of a Byzantine musico-poetic text today aims at bringing out its basic forms/versions,
in each case relying on a dux, as well as at the concise presentation of the various variants from
the remaining manuscripts, in one or more critical apparatuses. lviii

## 4. Attempts to Decipher Ekphonetic Notation

## 4.1. The General Framework

We saw in the preceding sections that, with the help of the Byzantine catalogues–didactic poems on ekphonetic notation, we can identify by name the various pairs of signs that occur in the Gospels, the Apostolos, and the Prophetologia, and observe the frequency of their occurrence, as well as their relationship to the biblical text.
Long engagement with ekphonetic notation led S. Martani to propose a revision of the way in which palaeographers analyse ekphonetic notation. More specifically, she proposes distinguishing between structural and ornamental neumes ('structural' and 'ornamental' neumes). The first category includes signs that display a high degree of stability in the way they are used and provide the framework of the recitation, while the second category includes signs that alternate more frequently, perhaps depending on local traditions or on the level of festivity

(e.g. the parakletike [παρακλητική]).lxvi
Constantine Psachoslxvii relates Byzantine emmelic recitation (εμμελής απαγγελία) to the manner of movement of the

so-called middle voice, of which Aristides Quintilianus (probably late 3rd–early 4th c.

AD) speaks, noting that it was in use for the recitation of poems and combined elements from the manner of

movement both of the spoken voice and of the melodic voice:
«As for voice (movement: Winnington-Ingram), one kind is by nature simple, and another not simple;
and of the latter one is continuous, another intervallic, and another middle.
Continuous voice, then, is that which produces relaxations and tensions imperceptibly because of a certain speed,
while intervallic voice is that which has its pitches manifest, but the intervening elements (instead of μεταξύ, the manuscripts have: μέτρα)
imperceptible; and middle voice is that composed of both.
The continuous, then, is that in which we converse,
the middle that in which we perform the readings of poems,
and the intervallic is that which, between the simple voices, produces definite intervals and single notes, and is also called melodic.»lxviii
On the modes, styles, and ethos of reading during the late Byzantine period, we can gather some information from Saint Gregory of Sinai (ca. 1255/1265?–after 1337). This leading representative of Hesychasm, first referring to cases/excesses that probably occurred at that time in various ecclesiastical settings, then sketches the ideal manner of reading for hesychast monks, which leads to the strengthening of the mind and of prayer:
«Let your reading be in solitude,
not with a voice of boastful pomp,
not in a rush of elaborately wrought eloquence
or with elegance of speech
or with the pleasure of melody,
or, though absent as if present, being insensibly despoiled by passion for the pleasing of certain people,
nor again insatiably, because “measure in all things is best”;
nor harshly,
nor sluggishly and carelessly,
but solemnly,
moderately,
steadily,
prudently,
rhythmically,
and noetically and psychically, or also rationally.»lxix

the signs oxeia (οξεία) and kremaste are interpreted as an ascending voice (Pa, the latter with dynamic emphasis),

## 5. The Present-Day Practice of Recitation

From the preceding sections it becomes apparent that thorough knowledge of the transmitted manner of emmelic recitation now constitutes a basic prerequisite, an indispensable “aural compass” for the in-depth study of ekphonetic notation.
Nevertheless, in recent decades a decline has been observed in the practice of emmelic recitation, since, on the one hand, the number of churches in which the Gospel pericope in the Divine Liturgy is no longer recited emmelically but simply read is increasing, and, on the other, there are more and more cases of improvisatory recitative in scales not customary in Greek ecclesiastical music (Western influences, such as the “minor,” or eastern influences, e.g. recitation in various maqams).lxxviii
In an effort to address this situation, Greek scholars and cantors devoted a range of papers in 2001 to “ekphonetic notation and practice,” with reference to the Prophetologion, the Apostolos, the Gospel, as well as to other texts recited in the services of the Orthodox Church, such as the kontakion, various prayers, and litanies.lxxix In the same year (2001), Antonios Alygizakis’s paper entitled “The Ekphonetic Psaltic Practice: the ‘Plain’ and the ‘Ekphōnōs’ Readings” was also published; it offers a “map” of the status quo of present-day ekphonetic practice and a new proposal for its notation. Some central points of the aforementioned paper are presented briefly below:

1. An important desideratum in the study of ekphonetic notation is the clarification of its relationship to more recent ekphonetic practice.lxxx

2. The “ekphonetic melos” constitutes an integral element of the Divine Liturgy (readings, exclamations, the old Leitourgika, the In Thee Rejoiceth of the Divine Liturgy of Basil the Great, etc.), of the Akathist Hymn (the oikoi of the Akathist, the prayers Spotless, Undefiled – And Grant Us), of the services of the daily cycle (Vespers, Orthros: exclamations, litanies, the Eothinon Gospel, etc.), of occasional services (Blessing of Waters, Mysteries, Funeral Service), of the services of Holy Week (e.g. the twelve Gospels of the Service of the Passion, Today He Is Suspended, the Hymn of the Three Children), of the Great Hours, of the Parakleses (e.g. during the period around the Fifteenth of August), etc.lxxxi

rhetorical texts—remain emphatically in the listener’s ear, create the framework for “clearly
stable, simple, and alternating melodic patterns.”lxxxix
All the above elements give this manner of emmelic recitation an exemplary
character.
In conclusion, we see that ekphonetic notation and the practice of emmelic recitation
present exceptional musicological and artistic interest. With roots in the musicality of the
ancient Greek language, in the ancient Greek prosodic signs and in the punctuation systems of the
Alexandrian grammarians, in the readings of the Old Testament in the synagogues during the Hellenistic
period, as well as in the rhetorical, epic, and theatrical art of Late Antiquity, a
compact graphic system was created in Byzantium, documented in MSS chiefly of the ninth to fourteenth centuries and
reaching the height of its development during the eleventh to twelfth centuries. While this graphic system declines
during the late Byzantine period and disappears in the post-Byzantine years, ekphonetic practice
is transmitted uninterruptedly on the oral level down to our own day, where a new
way of notating present-day ekphonetic practice with the signs of the New Method is also proposed, for teaching
purposes.
Byzantine lists—didactic ekphonetic pericopes—allow us to identify the old
signs and to trace the way they functioned. Taking these lists as a point of departure, during the first
half of the twentieth century the systematic rediscovery of ekphonetic notation began, along with persistent work on
the manuscripts and the preparation of critical editions (Copenhagen School). The systematic correlation of
palaeographical research with the study of transmitted ekphonetic practice in all its manifestations,
as well as with the tracing of modes of reading in Byzantium, promises much, both for a deeper
understanding of Byzantine ekphonetic notation and for the support and safeguarding of
present-day ekphonetic practice.

One translation of this passage might be as follows: “The voice can be either simple or not simple;
and the latter has the following subcategories: continuous, intervallic, and intermediate.
The continuous voice is that which makes tensions and relaxations (risings and fallings) imperceptible, because of
speed;
the intervallic voice is that which does indeed have manifest pitches, but the spaces between them are not apparent;
the intermediate voice is that which has elements of both the preceding subcategories.
The continuous voice, then, is that which we use when we speak;
the intermediate voice is that in which we read poems;
and the intervallic voice is that which produces, between the simple voices, intervals (leaps) of a definite size
and stops (on various degrees); this is also called the melodic voice.”

lxxi The methodology followed by Høeg was the following:

a. The approximate transcription of the didactic passage from the Sinai MS, on the basis of the Palaeo-Byzantine
