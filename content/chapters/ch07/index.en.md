## Summary

This chapter deals with Middle Byzantine notation, an intervallic musical script in use for approximately 700 years (mid-12th to mid-19th century), which arose from Old Byzantine Hagiopolite notation and was followed by the New System. Its long course may be subdivided into various evolutionary phases, the best known being the fully developed, the late, and the exegetical phase, which prepares the work of the Reformers of 1814–15. The tracing of the signs of Middle Byzantine notation is first addressed through the study of the Protheoria (Προθεωρία) of the Papadike, in two versions: the Protopapadike of Neophytos Hieromonk from Damascus, Petropolitanus gr. 495, and a late form of the Protheoria of the Papadike from an autograph by Demetrios Lotos, Protopsaltes of Smyrna, ms. Holy Monastery of Docheiariou 338. Subsequently, the sounded and unsounded signs of Middle Byzantine notation are presented in the form of multicoloured tables, as an aid for their systematic learning by readers. The chapter continues with the presentation of a methodology for the first steps in studying manuscripts in Middle Byzantine notation, through the transfer of the sounded signs to the staff or into an alphabetic system. The study of Middle Byzantine notation continues in the following chapters through to the end of the book.

## 1. Prolegomena

## 1.1. Dating and Some Milestones

Middle Byzantine notation, more generally known also as the Old System, was in use for approximately seven
centuries. It evolved out of the final stage of Old Byzantine Hagiopolitan
notation (Coislin VI), probably during the first half of the twelfth century (Papathanassiou, 1997, p. 57).
Among the oldest specimens of this notation known to us today are two stichera
in the Patmian manuscript 218, which had been sent in the year 1166 to the Holy Monastery of Saint John the
Theologian on Patmos and otherwise contains stichera in Coislin V and VI: Figure 7.1.
The historical course of Middle Byzantine notation, which spread throughout the
Byzantine Empire and developed further in the post-Byzantine period in Greek and
foreign-language manuscripts (Romanian, Slavic, etc.), comes to an end around the middle of the nineteenth century,
when the analytical notation of the New Method definitively prevails.

## 1.2. Significance and Main Characteristic

Between Old Byzantine and Middle Byzantine notation there intervenes a paradigm shift
in the history of notation (Ger. notationsgeschichtlicher Paradigmenwechsel).v
This change concerns the mode of encoding and the clarity of the musical information that
the new notation conveys in relation to the older. More specifically, Constantine
Floros emphasizes that
“In contrast to the Old Byzantine notations, Middle Byzantine
notation is diastematic. This means that it indicates the
intervallic relationships with great precision. This is not achieved with the aid
of a system represented by lines, but with the aid of signs that
determine its intervallic relationships. With the aid of specific
rules that govern these relationships, there is revealed in this notation
an astonishing transparency and an enormous wealth of representational shaping
and flexibility.”vi

309

The significance of this change was also highlighted by Oliver Strunk, father of
American Byzantine musicology, who stressed that from the period in which
Middle Byzantine notation took shape during the twelfth century,vii “and until the time of Chrysanthos, whose
first publication appeared in 1821, the notation used in the
tradition of Byzantine chant underwent no radical change. With the general acceptance
of the principle of diastematicity right from the beginning of this long
period, Byzantine musicians had taken a crucial step (a crucial step), a step
no less crucial than the corresponding one taken in the West a little earlier”
(the reference is to Guido d'Arezzo, ca. 1030). “And once this crucial step had been taken,
there was no longer any possibility of return. In the light of this historical moment, the
notational innovations of John Koukouzeles and his fellow maistores
and their successors are revealed as mere expansions of an already secured
system, prompted by the ever greater and more urgent
needs of a rapidly evolving musical style.”viii

## 1.3. Phases of Development

Although the next radical paradigm shift for Byzantine notation would take place with the
New Method in 1814/15, Middle Byzantine notation nevertheless underwent a quiet and continuous development between the twelfth and the beginning of the
nineteenth century. Research into this evolutionary course
has continued from the end of the nineteenth century to the present day, without yet having been completed. For this reason,
the various proposals mentioned below for division into individual phases in the development of
notation within these seven centuries constitute epistemological
tools still in formation, open to additions and revisions as research
on this subject advances in the future.
The palaeographical research conducted during the twentieth century and the beginning of the twenty-first century revealed the
fact that both Old Byzantine and Middle Byzantine notation change not only
diachronically but also synchronically, depending on the repertory being recorded at any given time.ix In
general terms, however, we could for the time being retain the following subcategories with regard
to Middle Byzantine notation, which is known in foreign-language bibliography also as
Round Notation:x

• Transitional, between Old Byzantine Hagiopolitan (Coislin VI) and Middle Byzantine (twelfth

century),

- early, developed, and fully developed Middle Byzantine notation (twelfth-fifteenth/sixteenth century),

- late Middle Byzantine notation (first half of the fourteenth-ca. mid-nineteenth century), especially formerly

known also as Kukuzelian notation.
For Middle Byzantine notation from 1175 to 1670 the term
Middle Full Byzantine Notation (Gr. Stathis) is also used.

• Exegetical Middle Byzantine notation (ca. 1670-1814/15 and in some manuscripts until the middle of the

nineteenth century). The exegetical use of MB notation, which appears in various, ever more numerous
manuscripts from 1670 until the Reform of the Three Teachers, may be
regarded—because of its particular importance for the more analytical recording of melos—and
as a distinct evolutionary period, under the name Transitional Exegetical Notation (Gr.
Stathis).

A rough sketch of the general characteristics of Middle Byzantine notation over the
course of the centuries could include the following:

 It is a diastematic notation, with various degrees of shorthand compression. This notation

shows clearly, through its so-called phonetic signs (emphona), the structural intervals of a
piece, what we may call the metrophonic structure of a troparion/hymn. Elements that
concerned the musical shaping of the piece (rhythm, embellishments at various levels, expression)
were realized according to the then-current performance practice of the Psaltic Art and were transmitted at the
written level through the great hypostases of cheironomy, and at the practical level through
cheironomy (the artful direction of the choir of chanters) and the art of oral musical exegesis.

310

- The musical and notational unit is not the isolated sign, but consists of the

sung syllable, since music is born from the text, according to E. Jammers.xi
Various theseis (θέσεις), that is, melodic figures with a characteristic structure
(intervals recorded with the phonetic signs and the ison), as well as with
a characteristic performance (rhythm, embellishments, expression), are adapted to the hymnographic text.
Many theseis are identified with special names in music-theoretical writings
(e.g. parakletike [παρακλητική], ouranisma [οὐράνισμα], etc.).

 As mentioned above, the boundaries between the individual stages of development of MB notation are not

absolute. The notation changes not only with the passage of time, but can also
differ among the various genera of Byzantine chant composition (e.g. the MB notation of the
Psaltikon includes in its soloistic chants certain special signs that do not appear in the other
musical collections). Nevertheless, we can observe some general tendencies:

- At the beginning, MB notation is very spare, especially when compared with the final stages of Old Byzantine

Athonite notation (Chartres III and IV).

- During the period of Kalophony (second half of the thirteenth-fifteenth century), with the contribution of St John Koukouzeles and

the other maistores, MB notation begins gradually to be enriched with various great
signs (the so-called aphona, or great hypostases, or signs of cheironomy), many of which
are written in red ink).
Some of these derive from Old Byzantine notation (especially from the Athonite). The difference between
fully developed MB notation and late MB notation lies in the increased number of aphonous signs
in the latter. The density of use of the great signs (in simple form or in various combinations
of aphona) increases ever more during the post-Byzantine period and reaches its peak during
the second half of the seventeenth-first half of the eighteenth century (First Flourishing after the Fall, or Renaissance of Byzantine music
[Chatzigiakoumis, 1999, pp. 41-66]).

 Already from the beginning of the sixteenth century there are written testimonies to the phenomenon of musical exegesis at the

theoretical level (Akakios Chalkeopoulos). Early stages of exegesis are also attested in
Slavic manuscripts with Kievan or partes notation, chiefly during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (see chap.

11). A long oral tradition of exegesis precedes this, perhaps from the fourteenth centuryxii or even

earlier. From around the year 1670 onward there is an intense effort within Byzantine
notation to give graphic form to the until then oral exegesis, through the ever more
analytical recording of the melos (= sonic result), especially of the papadic genus, and later
of other genera as well (sticheraric and heirmologic). Thus many more intervallic
signs (the so-called emphona and the ison) are used for the more accurate recording of the melodic movement of the
theseis, beyond their structural pitches, while by contrast many of the great signs are set aside,
since their melic content was recorded in greater detail.
On the basis of the exegetical tradition, MB notation appears as a refined and multilayered
shorthand system, in which the signs cannot be interpreted in isolation, but in relation to
their context (mode, melodic degree, genus, species, style, level of festivity, manner of performance
chorally or soloistically, poetic text, etc.). For the above reasons, D. Giannou called this
notation a 'context sensitive notation'.xiii Exegesis, that is, the traditional mode of
decoding the notation, may have various types/levels (“slow,” “short,”
even “syllabic” or “mixed” [Arvanitis]).xiv

 The Reform of 1814/15, which brings a second paradigm shift in the development of

notation, comes in essence as the culmination of a long series of efforts
oriented toward the simplification and perfection of MB notation, with the ultimate aim of the
analytical recording of the melodies and their easier teaching.

## 2. Tracing Middle Byzantine Notation - The Protheoria of the Papadike

The principal didactic music-theoretical text during the period in which Middle Byzantine
notation was in use was the so-called Protheoria of the Papadike.
The Protheoria of the Papadike, known in foreign bibliography simply as the Papadike, constituted
a flexible collection of short texts, tables, and didactic poems, all of which together served
as the basis of the musical preliminary training of chanters from the thirteenth to the beginning of the nineteenth century.

311

In other words, it was a corpus of notes, which was shaped together with the needs of
teaching, in relation to the development of the repertory. The Protheoria of the Papadike also constitutes
a reliable mirror of the development of Middle Byzantine notation and, more generally, of
music-theoretical thought.
The Protheoria has an extremely rich written tradition. There are, however, no two identical copies
of this text. Nevertheless, certain main categories and various versions
of them can be distinguished:

• A. The Protopapadike of the first developmental stages of Middle Byzantine notation,

versions of which were included in old Sticheraria, and

• B. the Protheoriai of the Papadike, which reflect and regulate late Middle Byzantine

notation. These were formed chiefly during the period of Kalophony, with the contribution of
maistores such as, for example, John Glykys, Saint John Koukouzeles, Xenos
Korones, John Kladas, Gregory Bounes Alyates, and they exist in a great
variety of versions, from shorter ones (3 folios) to very extensive ones (30 folios and more).
They are usually found at the beginning of codices of the Papadike or Anthology of the Papadike type.
During the post-Byzantine period, on the one hand the bulky Protheoriai are copied, and in part
enriched, sometimes assuming a monumental character; on the other hand there is also created

• C. a shorter version, with the most basic elements for preliminary training, which is also placed

at the beginning of Anastasimatarion manuscripts.

Below and in the next three chapters we shall examine various Protheoriai of the Papadike, for
the learning of the basic elements concerning Middle Byzantine notation.

## 2.1. The Protopapadikexv

The term Protopapadike was proposed by the Danish Byzantinomusicologist Christian Troelsgård, for the naming
of the older versions of the Protheoria, which accompany the teaching and the developmental course
of Middle Byzantine notation in its first stages. The various versions of the Protopapadike
deal in a very summary way with subjects concerning: a. the oktoechos (martyriai, lists
of apechemata) and/or b. the sign-sounds (sēmadophōna) of Middle Byzantine notation (categorization of signs and
corresponding lists, intervallic-metrophonic value of the phonetic signs, theseis).
Various versions of the Protopapadike have so far been identified in the following manuscripts:

• At the end of the Sticherarion of the Holy Monastery of St Catherine at Sinai, no. 1218 of the year 1177, fol. 271r, a list

of apechemata, by the main scribe, Nikephoros the Priest (Raasted), or by a later hand (thirteenth century,
Harlfinger et.al.),xvi

• in the St Petersburg fragment 495, which, according to Porphyrius Uspensky,

comes from the Sticherarion of Neophytos Hieromonk from Damascus (first half of the thirteenth century), fols. 1v-4v.
By the hand of Neophytos hieromonk (and domestikos) are also the parallagai of echemata in the Sticherarion
Sinai 1231 (year 1235/36), as well as the table with the metrophonic value of the signs of Middle Byzantine
notation in the manuscript of the Holy and Great Monastery of Vatopedi 1492 (year 1241/42). xvii

• In the Sticherarion Parisinus gr. 261 (year 1289), fols. 139v-140v: a list of signs of the papadic art,

categorization of the signs into phonetic and melic signs, the martyriai of the eight modes, with corresponding names
of ancient Greek harmoniai.xviii

## 3.2. The Aphonic Signs of Middle Byzantine Notation: a First Look

The voiced signs were those neumes of Middle Byzantine notation that were related
primarily to the structural level of a piece, i.e. to its quantitative dimension (Ger. Struktursinn),
measured in phōnai. The great signs constituted the category of signs that concerned
the qualitative substance of the piece, the level of musical performance (Ger. Aufführungssinn).xxx
The operations of the great signs concern:

-  rhythm,

-  various levels of embellishment and/or transformation of the metrophonic structure of the piece, which

is recorded with the ison and the voiced signs, as well as

 musical expression.

The principal aid to psaltic performance in Byzantine times and until approximately the first half of the 17th
century was the art of conducting, which was called cheironomia.
Whereas the Protopapadike of Neophytos Hieromonk (Petr. gr. 495), which presents an early
Middle Byzantine categorization of signs, presents only 17 shapes in the group of symphōna, the
corresponding section of great signs in the late Middle Byzantine categorization of signs
usually includes around 40 shapes, together with their names. The list of great signs
always begins with the ison. The reason is explained to us by the first paragraph of the Protheoriai, which in ms.
EBE 2401 has the following wording (see Figure 7.8):

![Description](image_9574.jpeg)

Figure 7.8. The beginning of the Protheoria of the Papadike from ms. EBE 2401, 15th c., fol. 11r. © National Library of
Greece.

From the paragraph on the ison it becomes clear that the concept of the aphonic in the theory of the Psaltic Art does not
concern elements that are not heard, but aspects of musical becoming that are indeed heard,
but are not measured by the unit of the phōnē (interval of a second). In other words, the aphonic signs
are placed beyond the basic calculation of phōnai, which constitute the melodic skeleton of a
piece and are recorded by the voiced signs.

## 4. Methodology for the Study of Middle Byzantine Notation

Once the signs of Middle Byzantine notation have been learned, the
palaeographical study of individual troparia and hymns may begin, in two cycles:

1. First acquaintance with the piece, for which the following working steps are proposed:

A. General overview of the piece:

-  Reading and understanding the poetic text,

-  division into musico-poetic units, based on the meaning of the text and with the help of punctuation marks

and martyrias.

1. Study by musico-poetic phrase:

-  Copying the text and signs, with their simultaneous identification,

-  chanting the isons and the voiced signs of the piece, on the basis of which the

intervallic skeleton or, in other words, the metrophonic structure of the specific troparion or hymn is recorded. Alongside
the chanting, and in order to facilitate the palaeographical study of the old notation,
a transfer of the intervals may be made below each musico-poetic phrase onto the staff or
into a special alphabetic system, as shown in the following sections.
II. Deepening the study of the piece by seeking its musical exegesis in the transcriptions
of the Three Teachers and other exegetes, a topic that will concern us mainly in chapters
10 and 11.

However, the maturation of musicological reflection on the importance of oral tradition in the study of
old Byzantine music, and the recognition of various degrees of stenographic character in it, gradually led
to a reconsideration of the entire transcription project (Raasted,
1986) and to a preference for alphabetic transnotation as a research tool. From the
multidimensional Middle Byzantine notation, only the ison signs and the
sounded signs are transferred into the alphabetic system, on the basis of the teaching of the Protheoria of the Papadike; that is, only the backbone of the
piece and not its melos, while the elements that concerned the level of performance (rhythm, embellishment
at various levels, expression) are left out, since they were transmitted through the oral tradition of the
period.xxxv The alphabetic form of transnotation is practical, occupying only a little space and
at the same time facilitating musicological analyses with the aid of computers (e.g. statistical
analyses of the number of occurrences of various pitches or formulae, etc.).
From the last decade of the twentieth century, however, a new, open form of
staff transnotation began to prevail, less abstract than the alphabetic one, in which only the pitches
are indicated on the staff, with black notes. For the major lengthening signs, white
notes are used conventionally. As in alphabetic transnotation, neither the rhythm of
Middle Byzantine notation nor the remaining elements that concerned the level of performance are
transferred into staff notation. Various scholars add legati to indicate groups of pitches that together form various
melodic theseis (e.g. the tromikon, etc.: see Troelsgård, 2011).

## 5. Recapitulation - Conclusions

In the present chapter a brief introduction to Middle Byzantine notation was given.
Topics were presented concerning:

-  the signs (sounded and unsounded),

-  the methodology for the study of Middle Byzantine notation,

-  the types of transnotation (staff and alphabetic) and their basic principles.

Nevertheless, these are only the first steps of study. In the following chapters we shall seek
evidence concerning the oktoechos and the level of performance.

348

It must be emphasized that the two types of transnotation presented in Figures 7.17-
18, the alphabetic and the open staff type, concern only the level of the structure of the pieces,
their quantitative dimension. They leave open all the elements that were transmitted viva voce and
concerned the qualitative dimension of the pieces: exact sizes of intervals, unwritten
changes, rhythm, melodic developments of the formulae (theseis) - embellishments of various levels, dynamics, articulation,
timbre, phrasing, ethos. In other words, only the metrophonic structure of the piece is transnotated
(what is recorded with the ison and the sounded signs), and not its melos (sonic result).
Consequently, transnotations do not serve as scores for musical performance, but
serve scholarly purposes, being tools in the hands of the musicologist that
facilitate the comparison of sources and his analytical-morphological and stylistic inquiries.
